# Controls are priced before they are built

A control is anything that checks, refuses, requires, warns or adds a step to someone's work: a test, a gate, a hook, a review stage, a checklist line, a rule seats must follow. It costs something every time it runs and every time someone has to satisfy it; its benefit is only the faults it catches that would otherwise have cost more. The owner, 2026-10-29: "It should be part of the shared harness that you don't build any controls without comparing their costs and benefits" [the owner's message of 2026-10-29]. The evidence below is in the author's record of that review: another project's record, the literature (`literature.md`) and the yield of another project's build stages (`build_pipeline_yield.md`). In three months another project's test functions rose from 261 to 4,446, mostly checking its own harness; 60 of 1,948 commit and acceptance tests compared the model with a sourced value, and its costliest failure (five production systems switched off behind a stand-in harvest reported as matching the sources) passed every check.

## Before a control is built or widened

Its price is written where it lives, in its own header or the commit that adds it:

1. The fault it catches, with its cases in the record or the literature. A check is worth only the decisions it changes (Howard 1966); a check whose failure leads to no fix is pure cost.
2. Its benefit: how often it catches a fault that would otherwise escape, times what an escape costs less what catching it here costs.
3. Its cost: its time on the path of the work times how often it runs; its dollars; false alarms times the cost of clearing one; upkeep, since it must change when the code does; the owner's time; and the behaviour it invites, since an agent judged on a measure works to the measure (Holmström and Milgrom 1991).
4. The cheaper alternatives, tried first: delete the rule or design that causes the fault; make the fault impossible by structure; a reminder before a refusal; one check where an escape becomes costly, riding a step that already runs; for a rule that would force rework, a rebuild or a stop, a reviewer's judgement instead (`peer_review.md` 3.2). People overlook removal unless asked for it (Adams et al. 2021), so the first question is always what to remove.
5. Its review date, and the reading that keeps it then.

It is built only where the benefit exceeds the cost on that evidence; an estimate is labelled as one. A check runs on every change of a class or on none of that class (Deming's all-or-none rule), except a small random audit share kept to measure what the check would find. The evidence on adding a second check of the same kind is mixed: in a randomised experiment more inspectors added delay, not detection (Porter et al. 1997), while a second code reader has raised a panel's recall at small cost.
A control lands with its dated keep-or-remove item on the roadmap (standards/process/roadmap.md).

Two exceptions. A check that keeps a record honest is not traded against cost (core rule 1; the owner, 2026-10-05: "The system must be honest with me no matter the token cost"), though its cost is cut where it can be and it is held to the false-alarm rule below like any other. A check that rarely fires stays if it guards a rare, costly fault, is accurate, and leads to action.

## What makes a check worth having

1. Independence. The seat that certifies work did not write it and cannot edit the check, and it can block (the bank supervisors' "effective challenge", SR 11-7). An agent grading its own work is a checker paid by the checked (Duflo et al. 2013), and language models do not improve their reasoning by self-correction alone (Huang et al. 2024).
2. Judged against reality, not against itself: an accounting identity, a closed form, a hand calculation at stated inputs, a sourced figure within a tolerance fixed before the run, or an independent reimplementation (as a spreadsheet and its Python twin are). Verification asks whether code does what was specified; validation asks whether the model is right for its purpose against data (Sargent 2013), and validation is what a result rests on: the error in Reinhart and Rogoff's spreadsheet was found by an independent replication (Herndon et al. 2014). A check on a stand-in quantity is itself checked against the outcome it stands for, as medicine learned from surrogate endpoints (Echt et al. 1991).
3. Able to fail. A check that passes before the code exists tests nothing new, unless the change is meant to preserve behaviour and says so. Meta keeps a machine-written test only if it fails on a deliberately broken version of the code (Alshahwan et al. 2024; Foster et al. 2025), and catching such planted faults tracks catching real ones (Just et al. 2014).
4. Hard to game. Agents special-case tests they cannot edit and fake success when they see no honest exit; an explicit way to report a task blocked (CORE 1.3) cuts that sharply (Zhong et al. 2025; Anthropic 2025).

## Tests

1. A test checks what the project is for, judged as above. It never checks internal details the design leaves free, wording or file layout, a tolerance tuned until it passes, or the bytes or numbers of an earlier output of the same code. A model under constant iteration, its speed work and refactors included, is checked by its stored suite (3a; CORE 1.4) and against reality (CORE 3.3), not against its own earlier numbers (the owner, 2026-11-20, in ieq-modeling: "no, please don't set before and after comparison tests. The model will be constantly updating and changing and I don't want endless delays because of anchoring to old bad numbers").
2. As few tests as decide the question. A test count, a coverage share or a pass rate is shown to reviewers and never made a target: suites generated to reach a coverage target often find fewer faults than random suites of the same size (Gay et al. 2015).
3. A test written to check a change is deleted when the change lands; once the code passes, it has done its work (the owner, 2026-11-05: "by default all the tests that are made to ensure good code just get deleted when the code lands properly"), unless a stored suite covers the code it changes (3a). A test's pass is evidence about the code, not about the world (CORE 1.1), so deleting it loses no evidence. It is deleted by default; it may be archived only where the seat is sure the archive will not recruit work, since git and the transcript archive already hold every test a seat wrote. This is the far end of regression-suite minimisation (Yoo and Harman 2012), which keeps the smallest suite that still catches the faults that matter: a suite kept for every change costs upkeep on every later change. A worry that outlives the change goes to 6.
3a. Code changed build after build keeps one stored suite, since a fresh check per build pays for its writing and its run every time (ieq-modeling, 2026-11-18 to 20: 298 minutes of check writing and 411 of reruns; the owner, 2026-11-20: 'There should be just one simple test suite stored for this that accommodates the changes'). The project's CLAUDE.md names it in one line: `Stored suite: <folder> covers <paths>; fast tier: <command>, under <N> s`.
3a.1. It runs in about a minute. The code runs at its smallest honest setting (a draw count read from the environment, with tolerances set from the Monte Carlo error at that count) and does no computation at import. The full run belongs to the run-level checks of 5, before a release.
3a.2. It holds only assertions that a correct change leaves true: identities, bounds, relations between runs (monotone, zero in gives zero out, scaling: metamorphic testing, `chen_2018_metamorphic`), sourced anchors, a twin's agreement, and the shape of generated text. An assertion that a deliberate change breaks is deleted, never re-pinned.
3a.3. It keeps the number of test functions it was approved with. A build adds or edits assertions inside those functions, or replaces one, and the test gate's net count holds the number there. The gate counts each literal parametrize case as a test function (`gate/test_gate.py:26`), so a check is added as an assertion inside an existing function or case, never as a new case (the seat's reading, from ieq-modeling's workflow seed-pass-0-2-1 of 2026-11-21, whose one new case had the gate refuse eight checkpoint commits).
3a.4. A suite is created only with the owner's approval (8), asked once for the suite.
3a.5. Where the owner has complained about a generator's output, its stored suite holds one assertion for each complaint a test can catch, named by the complaint's OW id, and a brief for that generator reads that list first; a complaint no test can catch is a rule line where the seat making the output reads it (his ask as relayed by the cost-benefit seat, 2026-11-20, not yet in a register; the form, with the suite as the register, is the seat's, after that project's the sheet generator's complaints register).
4. A failing test is a reading: the first question is whether the test or the code is the wrong part. A lasting test that fails gets a fate in the commit that answers it (Keeping 1): where the code is wrong, the code is fixed and the catch recorded; otherwise the test is deleted, never patched or skipped.
5. In a model project the checks that pay by default run on every model run, not every change: accounting identities and bounds a stand-in cannot pass (zero enrollment gives zero benefit; the present value of net benefits equals benefits less costs on every draw), a printed record of which mechanisms are on, stood in for or off, and speed against its target rather than against the last run. A check whose cost grows with the project's age, or a ratchet frozen at today's state, is not built.
6. A worry that outlives a change goes to the first home that holds it: a type or a single implementation that makes the fault impossible to write; a refusal where the bad value enters (CORE 3.1); a check read on every run (5); a lasting test, last. The first two are ordinary code, priced in their commit, and need no approval; they fire on real inputs, where a test fires only on its own (design by contract, Meyer 1992). A check meant to last, a run-level check or a test, is a control of its own, priced as one (above) and added only with the owner's approval through the test gate.
7. A lasting test builds every input itself and reads nothing else: no live case, stored source, other project's folder, git state, printed wording or file count. It compares the code with a figure the code did not produce (the section above, 2), and the commit that adds it names one planted fault and shows the test failing on it (the section above, 3). In the cost-benefit project every one of 42 recorded failures of its approved tests came from such a dependency and none from a fault in the code (the survey of 2026-11-14).
8. After a change has landed, a seat may ask the owner to keep a lasting test, one worry per message, saying which result would be wrong and by how much with nothing else showing it, why no earlier home in 6 holds it, the test's lines and seconds, and what covers the worry if the owner says no. The approval line quotes that question and the owner's reply; a reply to another question approves nothing, and the same survey found tests landed on such replies in all three projects. The owner [the owner's message of 2026-11-15]: "I hate censoring communications, and fear what happens if the desires that led to these tests are driven underground"; his "4a" chose this route.

## Review matched to risk

The depth of review follows what an error that got through would cost and how soon a cheap observation would show it: severity and detection, the two axes of FMEA (IEC 60812), as every field that tiers review does (DO-178C; ASME V&V 40). Risk-adapted monitoring of clinical trials matched full monitoring at under half the cost (Brosteanu et al. 2017, randomised). Two questions about the change set its review, for every change whoever makes it: a `build`, a `clerk`, or the seat's own hand edit (his 11a, 2026-11-21, choosing the seat's option text "A.5 Review sized by the two questions in 17.2.3, replacing the seat's own risk tier"; the questions are the R83 consultant's, the author's premortem note, section 4).

1. Would an error be silent at its first use: would the first run, or the first look at the output, fail to show it?
2. Does the file run in every session the moment it lands: a hook, an agent definition, a workflow, a rule file every open session loads? No one looks at such a file before it is used, so its error reaches every session at once (blast radius, after the Google SRE Workbook, cited from memory).

The answers set the route:

1. Neither: the maker runs the check that shows the change working (an existing test, the commit's type check, one look at the output) and the seat reads the diff. No build runs, since its coder and command runner would rerun a check the commit reruns anyway (the owner, 2026-10-30: "I am pretty sure I specifically asked for matching review to risk").
2. Either: one `code-reviewer` also reads the diff before it lands, about $2 to $4: a `build` at standard, or one `code-reviewer` dispatch for a hand edit or a `clerk` change.
3. Silent in numbers a reader relies on (code every result passes through, such as a model's accounting, discounting, sampling or input boundary, or a deliverable about to go to a client or reader): a `build` at high, which adds a check written by an agent other than the coder.

The answers, not the area a change lands in or a tier the dispatcher picks, decide the route, since a seat's own tier tells nothing: since 2026-11-14 the shared folder ran 93 of 96 builds at high, and builds with a fixed must-fix ran 26 of 93 there at high, 11 of 31 at standard, 3 of 20 at high and 0 of 11 at low in the cost-benefit project (the author's premortem note, section 4; the owner, 2026-11-21, cost-benefit register: "instances always choose too high of a risk and we need to teach them better"). The brief states the two answers in one line. The third route stays narrow, since a level most changes reach tells nobody anything, and trial monitors' triggers have been found not to pick out problem sites (Stenning et al. 2018). The build workflow records each change's route and findings, so the questions are checked against what review finds. What would change them (the consultant's thresholds): the separate check writer catching faults the reviewer missed in more than 1 run in 10 widens the third route; `clerk` changes to suite-covered code showing no faults after landing over 20 runs narrows the second.

A data edit that replaces a value with its source's figure goes through no build and no roadmap row: it is made directly, with its source beside it (the owner, 2026-10-27: "any future input number change ... just happens instead of going through any process"; [the owner's message of 2026-10-27]: "Filling starting granaries should have been changing a few numbers. Just doing it should have been easier than writing it into the roadmap!"; "with its source beside it" is the seat's). A number with no source is a research question, not a data edit (the seat's, from another project's memory `review-matches-risk-data-and-module-changes-just-happen`). What each build level runs is the shared build workflow's, `workflows/build.js`, whose header gives each stage.

No build reads its brief before the code is written, for every project (the scope is the seat's; the owner, 2026-11-16: "yes to a but make sure the checks get deleted after things land", choosing the seat's option a for the shared folder; decisions 31, sections 4 and 5). The check written by an agent other than the coder, its rerun and the reviewer of the finished diff stay. Until the review of 2026-12-01 a fault found after landing in a change built this way gets a row in the build ledger naming its run (`{"date", "run", "fault_after_landing"}`).

## Keeping and deleting

1. Every finding a control raises gets a fate, fixed or dismissed, so its yield can be counted. A project that records no fates can never price its reviews.
2. A check whose findings are dismissed more than one time in ten is fixed or switched off, the rule Google applies to its code analysers (Sadowski et al. 2015, 2018b). Every low-value warning lowers attention to the high-value ones (Drew et al. 2014; Ancker et al. 2017).
3. At its review date a control stays only where the record shows it caught more than it cost; removal is the default.
4. A refusal helps well-meaning seats avoid mistakes; it is never a wall against an adversary. A reminder comes before it, it names a route the refused seat holds, and a refusal the seat can route around with the same effect is deleted. Detection is different: a check that an agent could game is built so that gaming it fails (item 4 of the section above).
5. A lack in the process found mid-task (a stage no workflow runs, a step whose failure loses finished work, a check that finds late what the record already knew) is fixed in the process, in the workflow, standard or agent definition, and the task then goes on through the fixed process; a hand workaround that finishes the task sooner leaves the lack for the next seat (the owner, 2026-11-02, in the cost-benefit project: "If you see anything that is a process lack, fix the process and dont route around it. The whole point of this is a demo to make the harness good"; that it holds in every project, since every project runs on the shared harness, is the seat's reading). The fix is priced like any other change to the process (above), so the first fix tried is a deletion.
