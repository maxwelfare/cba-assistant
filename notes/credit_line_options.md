## 1. What the norms are

1.1 Who is held to a disclosure rule. Every rule found concerns a generative AI tool used to produce content. None concerns software that only calculates, which is cited as a courtesy (see 1.6). The CBA Assistant writes text and builds models, so it falls under the AI rules, and the credit line is a disclosure first and publicity second.

1.2 Journals and editors. ICMJE: "the journal should require authors to disclose at submission whether they used AI-assisted technologies (such as LLMs, chatbots, or image creators) in the production of submitted work", describing "how they used it" in the cover letter "and the submitted work in the appropriate section if applicable"; and "Nondisclosure of AI use ... may be construed as misconduct in some circumstances" (`icmje_2025_ai`). Elsevier: a separate statement "immediately above the references", naming the tool, the reason and the author's oversight, in the form "the author(s) used [NAME OF TOOL / SERVICE] in order to [REASON]. After using this tool/service, the author(s) reviewed and edited the content as needed and take(s) full responsibility" (`elsevier_genai_journals`). Nature (earlier version): "Use of an LLM should be properly documented in the Methods section" (`nature_2025_ai_policy_archived`); its current page says only that authors "Disclose AI use for trust and transparency" (`nature_ai_policy`). APA Journals: "Disclose the use in the most relevant place in the manuscript, most likely the Introduction or Method sections", and a general note in the author note for copyediting (`apa_2026_policy_generative_ai`, `apa_2026_title_page`). COPE's position statement was refused by its site (403) and was not read; its content is taken here only as reported in the three sources above.

1.3 What it must not imply. ICMJE: AI tools "should not be listed as authors because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the work ... humans are responsible for any submitted material that included the use of AI-assisted technologies." Elsevier: the tool "do[es] not qualify for authorship". So the credit must sit outside the byline, say the tool assisted, and leave responsibility with the named people. It must also not suggest the tool's maker checked or endorses the result.

1.4 Government. Government of Canada's guide for federal users: "Clearly indicate that you have used generative AI to develop content" and "Don't consider generated content as authoritative" (`canada_2026_genai_guide`); it names no placement. The UK Generative AI Framework for HMG, fetched, asks for openness about where AI systems are used but sets no rule for marking a document; it is not cited.

1.5 Think tanks. Practice is looser than the journals'. Brookings: "Individuals should always disclose to their supervisor, reviewer, or editor if a work product has been created with the assistance of generative AI and whether it has been properly reviewed", with external disclosure required only for AI images and AI text quoted verbatim at length (`brookings_2024_genai_approach`). Stimson: internal disclosure always, and external disclosure "(in the acknowledgements, methodology, or other appropriate section) may be requested for substantial use of AI" (`stimson_2026_ai_publications`). The Institute for Public Relations, CIGI and PPIC were seen only in search summaries (CIGI and PPIC refused the fetch) and are not relied on. RAND's policy page refused the fetch. Finding: no major think tank was seen putting a tool credit on a byline or in a page footer; disclosure goes in acknowledgements or methodology, and many require it only for substantial use.

1.6 Software credit, separate from AI. Scholarly practice credits tools in a methods or acknowledgement sentence ("analysis used R 4.x"); the FORCE11 software citation principles (Smith et al. 2016) say so, but the paper's site refused the fetch, so that point is recalled, not checked.

1.7 Where, in summary. The convention is a labelled disclosure in or next to the methods or acknowledgements, stating tool, purpose and human responsibility. A byline footnote is not the convention and invites reading the tool as an author. A page footer is not found in the policies; it is a design choice that gives visibility but carries none of the content the policies ask for.

## 2. Recommended placement by document

2.1 One-page summary. One line of small type at the foot of the page, outside the byline and the body. Reason: there is no methods or acknowledgements section to hold it (1.2), and the Canadian rule asks only for a clear indication (1.4). Name the tool; leave responsibility to the cover of the full set.

2.2 Fast technical note. The same footer line on page one, plus a two-sentence "Method note" in its methods paragraph: the tool, what it did (listing effects, estimating, drafting), that no model code was run at this level, and that the named authors are responsible. Reason: it is the shortest document with a methods paragraph, where ICMJE, Nature and APA put the description (1.2).

2.3 Policy paper. A short acknowledgements paragraph, or the back of the title page where the house layout has a colophon, in the Elsevier form (tool, purpose, human responsibility), with the footer line on the title page only, not on every page. Reason: Stimson and Elsevier put the statement in an acknowledgements-type section (1.2, 1.5); a running footer on 20 pages adds visibility but no content and reads as advertising.

2.4 Technical appendix. A full methods section describing the harness and process (the owner's plan, the owner's message of 2026-11-10: "The full technical appendix will of course have a full section describing the harness and process"; the owner's message of 2026-11-13: "start the writeup of the methods section of the technical appendix, including the diagram and details of this whole process"), a one-sentence declaration in the Elsevier form directly above the references, and the footer line on the title page. Reason: ICMJE and Nature require the description of use in the methods; the Elsevier position makes the short statement findable.

2.5 Not recommended. A footnote on the author's or client's name: it puts the tool in the byline position that ICMJE and Elsevier reserve for accountable humans (1.3), and it is the placement a journal editor would be likeliest to query.

## 3. Candidate wordings (each under 15 words; the owner chooses)

3.1 "Produced with the maxwelfare.com CBA Assistant." Seven words; the owner's own, a colophon register, silent on what the tool did and on who is responsible.

3.2 "Prepared with the CBA Assistant (maxwelfare.com), an AI tool. Responsibility rests with the authors." Fourteen words; the policy register of ICMJE and Elsevier, longest but meets 1.3 on its own.

3.3 "Analysis supported by the maxwelfare.com CBA Assistant, an AI-assisted research tool." Twelve words; the acknowledgements register, says AI plainly and says "supported", so it reads as help, not authorship.

3.4 "Drafted and modelled with the maxwelfare.com CBA Assistant (AI); reviewed by the authors." Thirteen words; states purpose and review, the Elsevier form shortened; true only where a named person did review the work.

3.5 "Made with the maxwelfare.com CBA Assistant, an AI research tool." Ten words; plain and brisk, the tone of a software "made with" credit, more promotional than the others.

## 4. What a user must be able to do

4.1 Remove or reword the line for their organisation's rules. The line is the tool's, and some clients bar third-party marks or require a specific heading (Elsevier's title, an agency's own AI statement). The user takes it, rewords it, moves it, or drops the footer; this is the case's choice, recorded in `requirements.md`. Default on, since the owner asked for it (the owner's message of 2026-11-10: "make sure that anything it produces has a subtle but professional footnote saying that it helped with the work").

4.2 Keep the disclosure of use distinct from the credit. A credit line may be dropped; a document that the tool largely wrote should not go out with no disclosure of AI use anywhere, since ICMJE treats nondisclosure as possible misconduct and Brookings and Stimson require disclosure to reviewers and editors. The technical appendix's process section and a statement in the cover note (the internal disclosure of Brookings and Stimson) serve this. If the user insists on nothing at all, the tool should say once that their own publisher's or employer's policy may require a statement, then comply; the choice is theirs.

4.3 Edit the responsibility clause. Wordings 3.2 and 3.4 assert human responsibility or review; they must be true. The user can reword them to what happened, or use 3.1, 3.3 or 3.5 where the line is not to claim review.

4.4 The line must never claim that maxwelfare vouches for the result, and the tool should not add its name to the byline or the author field, per 1.3.
