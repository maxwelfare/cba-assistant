# The case author's Method 5: implementation options

Read by the `case-author` agent where its task adds the implementation options (`author_fast.md`, item 5); never sent as a message. The numbers are its definition's Method numbers. `<case>` is the case's folder name.

5. Implementation options, where your task says the case has them: by default where the policy's means of being carried out are not yet fixed, and wherever the case's requirements.md adds them (the scope-case skill's presets.md 10.3, row 4): concrete plans for carrying out the policy, each costed, with levels of enforcement among them; in the owner's words, "Not full legal code, but pseudocode that talks about concrete plans" (the owner's message of 2026-11-07). The method and its sources are decisions/how-a-case-makes-implementation-options.md.
5.1 Plans from three helpers. Once you have read the design, dispatch three helper agents in the background, each blind to the others and to your model, so that their plans do not follow your framing. Give each the same description of the policy from design.md (what changes, for whom, where, from when, by what instrument and under whose authority) and the requirements on its scope, and give each one standpoint: the agency that must carry it out and enforce it; those who must comply or who bear its costs; and someone who knows how comparable policies were carried out elsewhere. Give each, so that no kind of option is missed, the five ways in which options can differ: scope (who and what it covers), solution (the instrument), delivery (who carries it out), implementation (timing, phasing and enforcement) and funding. Ask each for two or three ways to carry it out, each a plan in numbered steps, not legal text. Each step names who acts, what they do and to whom, how many and how often, and what it needs (staff, money, data, a legal power); where a duty falls on someone, how it is enforced (inspection, reporting, penalties, information, or a price such as a fee or a tradable permit) and what share would comply, and why; when it starts and how it is phased in; and how someone could evade or game it. Each plan names the precedent it follows and its source, marked as recalled where the helper has not read it. The return ends with one line for each idea the helper considered and dropped, with its reason. Each prompt ends with the welfare paragraph of the shared standards/process/welfare.md, "At a dispatch". Write each return to cases/<case>/implementation/plans_<standpoint>.md, which your commits carry; the three returns are the long list.
5.2 The short list. Keep three options: the policy as the design states it, and, on the attribute that most changes net benefits, often enforcement, one stronger and one lighter. Add a fourth or fifth only where the plans differ on a second attribute that could move the result, such as the least that meets the policy's aim. Give each plan you do not keep one line with its reason. The design's base case is the comparison for all of them.
5.3 Costs. Each option kept has its own table, implementation/<option>.toml, in the form of estimates.toml, holding only its differences from the designed policy, whose own costs are already the case's estimates; steps all options share cancel in every difference. Cost each step that differs bottom up, as one estimate: the activity's unit cost, times how many there are, times how often each happens, times the share those affected would not do anyway. Keep the government's costs apart from those of the people who comply: the cost of complying grows with the share who comply, the cost of enforcing with the effort of enforcement. A fine or fee collected is a transfer between people, not a cost; count only the cost of collecting it. A step whose cost is a small part of its option's difference gets a wide judgement range in place of a search (the owner's rule 6).
5.4 Benefits, by the share of the effect realised. An option's share is the part of the designed policy's full effect it would realise, through those who comply, those who do more than the rule asks, and those deterred without being inspected. The designed policy's share is the one its estimates state or imply: an effect measured under a real policy already contains the compliance it saw; where they assume full compliance, the share is 1. An option's change in benefit is one estimate: the designed policy's benefits, at the sums of its benefit rows' lows, middles and highs, times the option's change in share as a fraction of the designed policy's share, of kind benefit for the stronger option and cost for the lighter. Each share's low, middle and high come from compliance observed under like enforcement (inspection, audit or retrospective records), with a second chain from the expected penalty against the cost of complying, marked as judgement; that reasoning alone may understate compliance where inspections and fines are rare, a puzzle the literature calls the Harrington paradox, though whether the evidence bears it out is disputed. The stronger option's share is above the designed policy's at each of its three values, and at most 1 unless there is evidence of compliance beyond the rule; the lighter's is below. Where the designed policy's share is at or near 1, take the stronger option on another attribute that could move net benefits, such as stringency or scope, or drop it and give the reason. A change in stringency or in who is covered changes the effect rows themselves, not the share.
5.5 The technical note gives the options a section: one line for each plan not kept; for each kept option, its plan cut to the steps that carry a cost or change the share, and the total tool's output on its table, its difference from the designed policy with its 90 per cent interval; and what the analysis advises: which option does best, under what conditions, and how far enforcement moves the benefits, saying that the option that looks best is favoured by its own errors of estimate (`smith_2006_optimizer`). The headline stays the designed policy; where another option does better, the note says so as a point for the user. The one-page summary gives the advice in a sentence or two.
5.6 These options stay at the fast tier when the draft is upgraded ("By default keep these to the fast-estimate track", the same entry).
