# The teaching sheet: a track whose product is one Google Sheet

A track beside the four levels of `.claude/skills/scope-case/presets.md` 10.1, not one of them. Its one product is a Google Sheet in the layout of the owner's Monte Carlo Template that teaches a named reader how the main effects of one policy are estimated; it writes no summary, paper or appendix. The owner's order (typed the evening of 2026-11-15 local time, 2026-11-16T03:19Z, the owner's message of 2026-11-16): "I need a sheet with 100 input variables or less. So it can't have the normal nudges to include everything. [...] we should have several tabs for the main effects of the rule (positive and negative), then several tabs for the initial scenarios, which draw data from the effects tabs, then a summary. [...] Don't run it through the normal review process, of course it will not be complete or publication ready. But it should be accurate and properly cited for the effects it does include." And of the inputs (the owner's message of 2026-11-16): "do proper lit searches for relevant inputs, if the literature has a distribution use that." The literature behind the rules is `research/teaching_sheet_practice.md`, cited as "practice" with its item number.

## 1 When it runs, and what the conversation settles

1.1 When a user asks for a teaching spreadsheet, a worked example in a sheet, or a small model to learn from. It may stand alone or follow a report on the same policy, whose files the designer then reads.

1.2 The requirements conversation (the `scope-case` skill) keeps its turns 1 and 2, its read-back and its record (0.8 to 0.10). It dispatches no listers, finder or estimators and shows no level card. In their place one turn settles, each item a requirement:
1.2.1 the reader: who they are, two or three things they know and two or three they confuse or do not know, each with one example question (the profile; practice 4.5);
1.2.2 two to four scenarios, versions of the policy or of the world that differ as real versions do, in scale, timing or who is covered (practice 1.7);
1.2.3 any effect the user wants in, or out;
1.2.4 the Google account that holds the sheet.
The frame is the default line of the skill's 0.5.8. The same turn gives the cost, about $35 at list prices in the one run measured (2026-11-16, its first build), and asks whether to start, or to see a draft first: every tab and column built as a sheet from rough values marked as rough, before any search. The owner, of his own first sheet: "give me a draft tab structure and calculation sketch [...] before diving in", and afterwards, "seeing the sheet made the mistakes more obvious" than the table in the chat had.

1.3 The cap of 100 input variables limits the effects, which keeps the sheet readable: a user who wants every effect is offered a level. It never limits how honestly an effect's inputs are entered (3.2.4): where the effects kept need more than 100 real inputs, the sheet goes above, and the return says so. The owner, 2026-11-16: "if we need to go over 100 inputs to do it right then go above".

2.4 The reader, a `novice-reader`, with section 5: writes `cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/novice_read.md`.
2.5 The designer, resumed: one revision (5.3), made in the data file; then the session builds the sheet again (4.4).

A print export or the grid's metadata is not a look, since neither shows a text cut off at the foot of its cell or running off the screen. The look is the script's (4.2.2), so the seat repairs nothing in the sheet (the owner, 2026-11-21: "the process when finding a flaw in anything generated shuold always be to fix the generator, never hand edit one of its outputs"), and reports each fault as a finding that names the part of the script, its layout files or the data file that made it and the fix there; a build of the generator carries it, and the sheet is written again by the normal route.
2.8 The session tells the user (section 7) and commits what no seat committed.

## 3 The designer: from the requirements to the spec

3.1 The main effects.
3.1.1 List the effects, good and bad, that the case's files and your knowledge name (`requirements.md`, any `effects/pooled.md` or report of the case, the policy's own impact analysis), one line each. The list is for choosing; no search lengthens it.
3.1.2 Size each in one line, an order of magnitude from two or three numbers you know, in the first scenario, marked as your judgement. It serves the choice and never enters the sheet.
3.1.3 Keep the effect the policy exists to produce, its main cost and each effect the requirements name, then the largest of the rest while their inputs fit the cap: usually three to six, one tab each. Leaving out the smallest loses the least (practice 3.2). The rest go to the omission list (3.5).

3.2 One input variable is a column whose row 4 holds one of the template's seven kinds (beta, constant, lognormal, normal, power law, triangular, uniform): any number the reader may change, a constant included. A calculation is not one.
3.2.1 A quantity more than one tab uses is entered once, on a Globals tab that follows the guide, and read by the others: it counts once and has one draw a row everywhere. The owner, 2026-11-16: "Probably many of them can be shared and put on a globals tab". Globals also holds the analysis year's own figures, such as the price index of the year every value is converted and discounted to. Everything else stays on the tab that uses it, beside its calculation; above all, the conversion of a past value from the literature into the analysis year's money (its year's price index and the ratio) sits right beside that value. The owner, 2026-11-16: "If you are converting values from sources don't spam up the globals, put the money adjustment calculation right next to that source. Globals should only be for things that will get reused, otherwise it's a huge attention tax to move between sheets", and "the current year analysis figure that things get discounted to is a global, converting things from past values in the lit is what needs to be beside them".
3.2.2 Each effect is a product of a few factors, about five at most, from one person and one event to its value per unit (the owner's rule 3; the `fermi-estimator` definition, item 1).
3.2.3 The spec numbers every input in one list (3.7), and the last number is the count. To come nearer it, move the smallest effect to the omission list. The count is never lowered by entering as one input a number worked out from others (3.2.4).
3.2.4 No calculation is entered as an input. The owner, 2026-11-16: "no calculation should ever be entered as a primitive or input. That is a completely fake and wrong way to reduce complexity". An input is a quantity a reader can look up: a statistic, a rate, a measured effect, a budget, a judged chance. A number made from other numbers (a projection from a base and a growth rate, a ratio of two statistics, a middle of several analogs, a parameter worked out from a source's other parameters) enters as its ingredients, each an input with its own source, and a calculation column the reader can follow. The owner, 2026-11-16: "make the fundamental inputs growth rate estimations themselves, not the output of a calculation. [...] make everything a real input and every calculation come from things that can be referenced". A published composite is an input only as its source states it. The steps of 3.4.1, from the interval a source reports to the template's parameters for the same quantity, are not such calculations: they restate one reported distribution in the template's form, and the citation shows the step.
3.2.5 Nothing uncertain is a constant. The owner, the same day: "There should be no constants anywhere for anything uncertain!" (his rule 1). The constant kind is for a definition and a unit conversion. Each quantity is one input column with one distribution of one continuous quantity (3.4), never a pick, on each draw, among "cases" or "routes" each with its own range, its chance and a random-number column, which spends five columns on one quantity and gives a distribution with two humps. The owner, 2026-11-16: "it used up 5 lines to make a goofy bimodal distribution. No real estimate is like that. Q should be a single distribution, probably a lognormal or possibly a beta. [...] make sure all future predictions are single distributions unless we have really good proof that we are dealing with separate attractors or regimes". A split into regimes rests on evidence, cited beside its column, that the world has separate regimes, and is put to the user as a decision of its own before it is built, never as a line inside a list. Two or more independent methods of estimating one quantity are a different thing, and keep the Combining Estimates form (3.3.4). A discount rate, like any quantity with a low, a middle and a high value, is one column with a distribution, followed by one chain of results. The owner, 2026-11-16: "do not have separate columns for low middle and high anything. make discount rate and anything like that a distribution. If we need to do a scenario analysis with one value we will set that to a single number, copy the table, and change it back. That's a lot better than spamming the sheet with redundant copies of things." The guide says that in a sentence or two (3.6.1). A date, a duration, a projection or a price that could be otherwise carries a distribution, and a time the result depends on (when an effect starts, how long it lasts, the year a value is taken at) is computed in each draw from such inputs, never fixed.

3.3 The search.
3.3.1 An input whose value rests on a class of studies (an effect size, a response, a measured rate) gets the logged search of `notes/briefs/author_search.md` 3.1 to 3.5, written to `cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/search/<class>/` in place of `cases/<case>/search/<input>/`. One search serves every input of its class. The folder holds the queries as run, the indexes, the counts, what was kept and what was read; the raw results of each search and chase stay in your scratchpad (`notes/briefs/author_search.md` 3.3).
3.3.2 A statistic comes from data first (`author_search.md` 3.0), an official value from the guidance of the frame, a definition or conversion from its source alone.
3.3.3 A `source-fetcher`, dispatched as the `case-author` definition's How to think 6 says, stores each source in the case's `sources/<key>/` before it is cited. A source the case already lists whose stored text is missing on this machine is fetched again in the same way, since a stored file is a convenience and never a requirement.
3.3.4 For the one input whose range moves the result most, where the search finds a second independent method (`author_search.md` 3.7.3.1 and 3.7.3.2), both enter in the template's Combining Estimates form at equal credence unless evidence says otherwise (the seat's default; the owner's rule 9 asks for a mixture, each draw picking one method by credence weight, and says nothing of equal credence), each method's columns a category of their own (3.6.5; the seat's layout). No `method-lister` runs.

3.4 The distribution.
3.4.1 Where the literature reports one for the quantity, the sheet uses it, in the matching template kind, and the spec writes the step from the quoted numbers to the parameters:
- an estimate with a 95 per cent interval symmetric on its own scale: normal, sd = (upper − lower) / 3.92;
- a ratio, or another positive quantity with an interval symmetric on the log scale: lognormal, which the template takes as mean and sd on the natural scale; with μ = ln(estimate) and σ = (ln upper − ln lower) / 3.92, mean = exp(μ + σ²/2) and sd = mean × √(exp(σ²) − 1), the inverse of the template's draw formula. Made up: an odds ratio of 1.3 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.5) gives mean 1.30, sd 0.10;
- a mean and standard deviation of a positive, skewed quantity: lognormal with those two;
- a share of r in n: beta, min 0, max 1, alpha r, beta n − r; from a mean m and standard error s, by the method of moments, alpha = m(m(1 − m)/s² − 1) and beta = (1 − m)(m(1 − m)/s² − 1);
- a tail the source fits as a power law: power law;
- a guidance value with a low and a high: triangular (low, value, high), as `cases/README.md` sets; with neither, constant.
The spread is the estimate's uncertainty, never the spread among people, since each row is one draw of the world; a meta-analysis's prediction interval for a new setting is preferred where reported (the seat's preference; the owner's rule 9 does not speak of prediction intervals).
3.4.2 Several studies of one kind and no pooled estimate: triangular from the lowest lower 95 per cent bound to the highest upper bound, mode at the median estimate, labelled as spanning the studies named (the triangular form and the mode at the median are the seat's; the owner's rule 6 asks only for a wide interval, unless the input drives the result).
3.4.3 No distribution reported, as for most forecasts: a judged distribution fitted to judged quantiles (O'Hagan et al. 2006, the SHELF method), a lognormal for a positive quantity with a long right tail and a beta for a bounded one. A median and one tail quantile fix a lognormal: μ = ln(median), σ = (ln of the 95th percentile − μ) / 1.645, then the template's mean and sd as in 3.4.1; a beta is fitted to two or three judged quantiles, with its bounds as min and max. Where the note (3.6.4) can say in one sentence which quantile the result rests on, it does. Row 3 begins "Judgment:", in the user's spelling, names who judged (the AI designer, or the user with their message's label) and gives the reason in a line; where only the spread is judged, it says which part is sourced.

3.5 The omission list: at most about ten lines, the largest first. Each effect not kept, with its direction and rough size; each simplification that loses information, such as one average for many places; a stream left out at the user's request, named so. It opens the reader's document (3.6.1), and the summary's title in A1 says that its totals cover the included effects only, by the rule that a headline names what it leaves out (`CLAUDE.md`, Scope, exclusions and honesty, rule 1; practice 3.3).

3.6 The lesson. The tabs run left to right in calculation order (practice 2.2), each with the script's header, one row one purpose: row 1 each column's name, with the tab's title in A1; row 2 a note for the column; row 3 an input's citation, or a calculation's formula shown as text without its row numbers; rows 4 to 11 what the user sets, the distribution and its parameters, with min, mode and max in rows 7, 8 and 9, yellow on an input, grey and empty on a calculation; rows 12 to 14 what the draws give for every column, in the same order: the low end of the interval whose width the guide sets, the mean, and the high end (the owner, 2026-11-16: "swap lines 8 and 9 so its min, mode, max. that has caused data entry errors in the past. And in the results too"); row 15 the histogram; row 16 on the draws (the owner, 2026-11-16: "people have been confused by the same block being used for input and output"). The spec adds no row. Every draw row holds its column's formula over the cells of its own row, with no array formula, and the guide says how to change one: edit row 16, copy it, select down to the last draw (Ctrl+Shift+Down) and paste (the owner, 2026-11-16: "The array formulas are an impediment to understanding"; FAST 3.03-09, "Do not construct array formulas"). A sheet that carries the script `tools/sheet_fill_down.gs` (Extensions, Apps Script) pastes an edited first draw row down by itself; an uploaded sheet carries none and the connector cannot write one, so the session installs it in the sheet's own Apps Script project through the user's browser, in the background, where it can drive one, and otherwise gives the user the file's text to paste there once. The session that installs `tools/sheet_fill_down.gs` in a sheet also appends the sentence " Or wait a few seconds for a script to do it for you." to the data file's Guide row on changing a formula, in the data file and never only in the sheet, so that `compare --every-draw` still passes and the next generate keeps it.
3.6.1 The guide, the data file's first tab, `kind = "guide"`, named Guide, is short. The generator writes its fixed parts itself, from text held in `tools/sheet_script.py`: the credit line (7.3); the template's own instructions, such as How to Read the Sheet, in the template's order, with the interval cell, how to change a draw row (3.6), how to run a scenario at one value (3.2.5), the worked example the data file names in `worked_example`, and Combining Estimates for each tab with methods, naming its label columns and the column that picks; his Useful References; the column widths; and the look. A sheet that carries `tools/sheet_fill_down.gs` says so with `fill_down_script = true`, which adds the sentence of 3.6 to the row on changing a formula. The data file supplies the rest, in order: what the sheet is and is not, in a line (7.1); a short "start here" block with two or three questions for the reader to explain, each saying where its answer is (practice 1.6); the tabs, one row a tab, the tab's title alone in the first cell, plain, and one or two short sentences in the next; the one-line general form each effect follows (practice 1.9); only the terms the profile marks unknown (practice 1.3, 1.5); the key, blue type for an input the reader may change (practice 2.6, 2.7); and one row linking the reader's document (`what`, `start_here`, `tabs`, `general_form`, `notes`, each a title and its text, `references`, added to his, and `details`). A text beginning `[AUTHOR:` is a placeholder, which `generate` writes and warns of. The guide is two columns of reading matter: column A, narrow, holds the titles and the section headings, the headings alone bold, and column B, wide and wrapped, holds all the body text, the template's instructions included; his Useful References, their labels and links, stand in the columns to the right of B. No row leaves a long text in a narrow column. The generator sets the guide's column widths to `GUIDE_WIDTHS` in `tools/sheet_script.py`, `[170, 600, 200, 320]` px. The owner, 2026-11-16: "change the blocks of text in Guide A to be split among columns so each sheet title can be bolded in its own column" (its bold replaced under his Decision 19, the owner's message of 2026-11-20, "a, my old order was confused", choosing the seat's option A: a tab's title alone bold); "and don't put sources in the guide, they only go in their columns."; "my instructions, e.g. 'how to read', should be before your wall of text on the tabs. And cut them down; if you want a paragraph of caveats make a linked google doc with the details." A source stands in its column's row 3 alone (3.6.4). The reader's document, `guide_details.md` beside the data file, holds what the guide leaves out and the reader still needs, in a page or two: the omission list (3.5), the caveats and any method the rows do not show. The session makes it a Google Doc in the account of 1.2.4 before the build, and the guide's row for it ends with the Doc's address.
3.6.2 The effects tabs, one an effect, each computing the effect's value per unit (per person, per household, per year). The first is the worked example, and the guide asks the reader to follow draw 1 across it (practice 1.1).
3.6.3 The scenario tabs: each reads every effect's value per unit, multiplies it by its own inputs (how many units, from when, for how long), applies a present-value factor (`fermi-estimator`, 6.4) and sums to the net benefits of the included effects, with the discount rate one input (3.2.5). Every scenario reads the same effect columns, so they share each draw. Scenarios are either alternatives, of which one happens, or branches of one future, each with its chance among its own inputs; where they are branches the spec says which exclude each other and which can coexist, and the summary adds them, each weighted by its chance, with a benefit two branches share counted once. The summary's columns, one for each scenario, read the scenarios' totals, and its title in A1 says they cover the effects in this sheet only (3.5).
3.6.4 Every column: a short name in row 1, two to five plain words in the manner of the template's names, with its unit in brackets where the unit is short. Its note, row 2, holds what the name and row 3 do not say: a calculation's formula in words ("Formula: children × risk × reduction"), an input's qualification, or where in the source its figure stands. The notes row shows up to four lines in the names' font, Calibri 9, and the reader clicks the cell for the rest, so a note's first 45 characters carry its point, such as what kind of thing it is ("Judged.", "Sourced.", "Method 1:", "Formula:"). A data column is 70 pixels wide and its name row holds five lines, so `generate` refuses a column name or a category's words (3.6.5) over 40 characters, a tab's title (3.7.1) over 45, and a word in any of them, a run without a space, over 11. A note that would only repeat the name or row 3 is left empty, as for a column read from another tab, and none begins with "=", which Google reads as a formula (the owner, 2026-11-16: "the 'read from' notes are redundant clutter, make notes be empty in these cases"). An input's row 3 holds its citation, the shortest passage that states its number, in quotation marks, and last the source's address, bare after the words, and nothing else; every input that rests on a source carries one, and the script writes it as a live link whose text is the words (the owner, 2026-11-16: "all of the reference links got dropped"). A cell of the guide that ends in an address is linked the same way. A calculation's ingredients sit just left of it; no cell comments (practice 1.4, 2.1, 2.4, 2.5). The sheet's words and units follow the usage of the user's country: for an American reader, American spelling and the unit names United States agencies use, such as "metric ton".
3.6.5 The categories. On every column tab the columns fall into categories a reader recognizes (the shared inputs, one stage of the chain, one method, the results), and each category's columns stand together. Before each category stands a narrow label column, its name cell holding the category's words in bold on bright yellow and its other cells empty, and the name cells of the category's columns share a pale tint, a different one for each category. The owner, 2026-11-16: "think about organization and presentation of variables, keeping categories together, and it's often helpful to have a label and a blank column to guide the reader, or at least have different colors for different categories"; the picture he gave of a sheet of his own has a narrow yellow column reading "Shared variables: Large Spread" before a category whose name cells are pale pink, and another before the next, whose name cells are pale yellow. Where a quantity is estimated by two or more methods (3.3.4), each method is a category named for it, and its label column shows the method's name, the word "Credence" and its credence number, and nothing else. The owner, the same day: "whenever you have multiple methods, [...] Have a column that's empty except for the method name and its credence number (and a 'credence' label) to help separate them. like in the past image I pasted". The data file writes both (3.7.1), and the script draws them.
3.6.6 The lesson's questions: three to six that a reader of the profile should answer after one read, each with the cells that answer it; the guide's questions are drawn from them.

3.7 The spec is two files in `cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/`, beside the reader's document (3.6.1). The sheet's columns are stated in the first alone; an input's quote stands in its citation there and in its Facts item, which 6.1.3 holds together.
3.7.1 `sheet.toml`, the data file, states the sheet itself: every tab in the sheet's order, and in it every column as one entry, in the form the header of `tools/sheet_script.py` gives. An input has its name, kind, parameters and citation; a calculation its name and its formula over the ids of its tab's columns, in any order, and another tab's columns cited directly as `Tab!id` or `'Tab name'!id` (`Globals!discount_rate`); any column may carry a `note` (3.6.4) and a `format`, one of four kinds of number: `"number"`, the default where none is given, `"$"`, `"%"` or `"year"`. For a kind, the script works out from the column's draws the decimals that show two significant figures of their typical size, and a year always a whole number with no separator; each of an input's parameters and of rows 12 to 14 shows two significant figures of its own value, and the stored value is never rounded (the owner, 2026-11-16: "try to keep numbers to 2 sigfigs when writing to the sheet and choosing the number format (although sometimes this won't work because it only lets you choose decimals)"). Use a kind, and write a literal Google number format, such as `$#,##0.0`, which the script applies as written, only where no kind can say what the column needs. A column whose numbers are large shows them in a scaled unit chosen from its values, one unit a column, named in words in the column's name and never by a letter in the cells (2,970,000,000 shows 3.0 under "Orders (billion)"; "(₩ Bil)" over 2,677,000 becomes "(₩ quadrillion)" over 2.7; R57 round 5), the data file's values unchanged (the owner, 2026-11-21T03:04Z and 03:07Z, transcript lines 173 and 229, not yet in the register; spellings corrected by the seat: "fix the generator so it lists numbers in million or billion or trillion when appropriate", and of "2.97 billion": "no that's 3 sigfigs"). Every column of a column tab carries `group = "<the category's words>"` (3.6.5), words within the limits of 3.6.4, every column of the tab grouped and each group's columns adjacent; a method of a tab is declared on it as `[[tab.method]]` with its `id`, its `group` (the category of its columns) and its `credence`, and a pick among methods cites `CREDENCE(<method id>)` in its formula, so no column copies a method's result to hold a credence and no column carries a credence of its own; a calculation that is one of the lesson's results, the designer's choice, carries `result = true`, which the script shows with its name bold; a column tab carries `about`, its title for A1: a title and not a sentence, within the limits of 3.6.4; the guide, the first tab with `kind = "guide"`, its fields (3.6.1), in which a text that ends in an address is a live link and `{col:...}` cites a column; `generate` refuses a file whose first tab is not the guide, and a guide whose `tabs` misses a tab or names one the file lacks. The tab named Globals holds what 3.2.1 puts there, and nothing about how the sheet works: that belongs in the guide. The guide carries `interval`, the share of each column's draws its rows 12 and 14 enclose (0.9 gives the 5th and 95th percentiles), which the generator writes in the yellow cell beside How to Read the Sheet, the cell every tab reads (the owner, 2026-11-16: "putting the 5 and 95 on globals in the normal column pattern is dumb, move them to a single value in the readme tab, and anything else that's how the sheet works rather than an actual CBA should go there"). No calculation draws a random number: one that picks among columns, such as the Combining Estimates block's, reads a uniform input column of its own, min 0 and max 1, placed just left of it, so that its formula shows only the pick (the owner, the same day: "remove the hash part of the formula on the combined estimates that grab 1 column"). Columns are cited by id and the script letters them, so a column added later moves no other entry. A column cited in any text (a note, a name, a citation, the guide) is written `{col:id}` or `{col:Tab name.id}`, never as a typed letter, which goes stale when a column is added.
3.7.2 `spec.md`, the lesson in prose: the sheet's link; the profile as the user gave it, never elaborated (practice 4.6); the lesson's questions; the effects kept and left out, with their sizes; a revision log; and a section `## Facts`, one numbered item an input, in the form `tools/quote_check.py` reads (`notes/briefs/facts_record.md` 3.1 and 3.2): its tab and column id, its quote with key and line, and the step to its parameters, or for a judged input who judged and why. Run `.venv/bin/python -m tools.quote_check cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/spec.md` until every quote passes.
3.7.3 Before the build, run `.venv/bin/python -m tools.sheet_script generate cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/sheet.toml --out <a scratch folder>` and read its `columns.tsv`: every column's mean and the two ends of its interval at the guide's setting, on the sheet's own 1,000 draws, the sizes the reader will see. Check each against the size you expected from its sources, and each total as the sum of its parts. A cause you name for a size that surprises you is tested before it is written anywhere.

3.8 The return: the paths of the spec's two files and the reader's document; the effects kept and left out; the input count; each search in one line (class, counts, kept); each input labelled judgement; the checker's count line.

## 4 The build

4.1 The sheet is built from the data file alone and uploaded whole into the account of 1.2.4, named for the policy. The owner's Monte Carlo Template gives the layout and the draw formula, which the script holds; the template is neither copied nor changed.

4.2 The sheet comes from the script, never from a model's typing of the spec (the owner, 2026-11-16: "you should be able to use scripts to make changes instead of doing things by hand"). `.venv/bin/python -m tools.sheet_script generate cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/sheet.toml --out <a scratch folder>` writes `sheet.xlsx`: every tab in the data file's order with the header of 3.6, each draw row a plain formula, the dropdown of the seven kinds on each input's row 4, the panes, the number formats and the look of 4.2.2. 4.3 The comparison. The session downloads the sheet as `.xlsx` (the Drive connector's `download_file_content`, export type `application/vnd.openxmlformats-officedocument.spreadsheetml.sheet`; the result names the file it saved) and runs `.venv/bin/python -m tools.sheet_script compare cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/sheet.toml <that file>`. The script computes every column in Python on the sheet's own draw formula and checks the final estimate Google computed: rows 12 to 14 (low, mean, high) of each column of the last column tab, the summary, each within 1e-7 of the column's largest value, since the sheet and Python share their draws (the script's docstring gives the reasoning). With `--every-draw` it compares everything instead: each column's name, note, kind and parameters, the citations and each tab's line, each calculation's row 3, the three rows of statistics, the guide's text, which cells of row 4 carry the dropdown, and the draws. ## 5 The novice read, and the one revision

5.1 The `novice-reader`'s task holds only the sheet's link, the spec's profile word for word, the lesson's questions and each tab's last column letter, so that it meets the sheet as its reader will (practice 4.4) and reads each tab to its end.
5.2 It returns numbered findings N1 onward, each a place in the sheet with what the reader would think there and what they lacked, and its prediction of the reader's answer to each question.
5.3 The designer answers each finding in the revision log, in a line: changed and how, or declined and why, and makes the changes in the data file. It makes a change that helps the profiled reader, and declines one that would add an effect, pass the cap or explain what the profile says the reader knows (practice 1.3). The reader is not dispatched again: one simulated reader is not a measure of real ones (practice 4.8).

## 6 The two checks, and what happens to a finding

The checker reads the spec's two files, the stored sources and the comparison's output (4.3), and changes none of them.

6.1 Each input against its source.
6.1.1 Run `.venv/bin/python -m tools.quote_check cases/<case>/teaching_sheet/spec.md`.
6.1.2 For each input, read the stored text at the quote's line and redo the step: the quote gives the starting number, the kind fits what the source reports (3.4), and the arithmetic holds to two significant figures.
6.1.3 The data file's entry for the input holds the Facts item's kind and parameters, and its citation the quote; a judged input names who judged.
6.1.4 The data file's entries whose kind is one of the seven, over every tab, number what the spec's count says; above 100, the spec says which effects' inputs took it there (1.3). No input's Facts show a value worked out from other numbers (3.2.4), and no constant holds an uncertain quantity (3.2.5).
6.2 Every formula.
6.2.1 Each calculation's formula in the data file computes what its name's formula in words says, from the columns it cites, and each read takes the column its name says.
6.2.2 The comparison exits 0 on an export taken after the last write: the sheet then computes, on every draw, what the data file's formulas say. The checker reruns it and reads its output; it reads no formula back from the sheet.
6.3 A finding is numbered C1 onward, names its input or column, and says whose it is: the sheet's, built otherwise than the spec; or the spec's, a quote that does not give the number, a step that does not hold, a formula that does not compute what its words say. A disagreement of the comparison is the sheet's, a fault of the data file or the script (4.3). The designer, resumed, fixes the spec's by correcting the value, relabelling the input as judgement (3.4.3) or moving it to the omission list, in the data file and the Facts, and the session builds the sheet again (4.4). The checker, resumed, checks each changed item again, until no finding stands.
6.4 The return: the input count, how many inputs and formulas passed, and each finding still open.
