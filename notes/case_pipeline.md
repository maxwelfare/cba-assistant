# Case pipeline: from the requirements conversation to the published document

Draft for the owner, 2026-10-31, version 2; section 3 cut to the single-author design on 2026-11-07. He asked: "design a process for making it easier next time, so future instances do not have to look up so much. I want a streamlined system of design conversation -> research->model->sheet twin->machine readable results->well designed pdf, with a handoff at each stage that makes things efficient. Nobody should have to search over the project folder and files for what they need." Version 2 answers his six points of the same morning: US and academic standards to cite ("DC people often don't like it when you cite foreign stuff"); onboarding for the design conversation, for a public harness whose user "has basic scientific and programming knowledge but is a novice at CBA"; a review of the model's structure before the case is written; a sheet that is easier to audit; the road from the zero draft to the next draft, through "a proper PRISMA lit review for the most important input variables" and "an expert elicitation pipeline for anything that can't be researched"; and "a google form link to email experts to collect the numbers, and once the results are collected it updates the google sheet and the python model".

## 1 The standards it follows

1.1 OMB Circular A-4 (2003, in force again under M-25-15; `omb_2003_circulara4` in `research/refs.toml`) sets the bar for the whole pipeline: "A good analysis should be transparent and your results must be reproducible. You should clearly set out the basic assumptions, methods, and data underlying the analysis and discuss the uncertainties associated with the estimates. A qualified third party reading the analysis should be able to understand the basic elements of your analysis and the way in which you developed your estimates." It asks for "peer-reviewed literature, where available", "the source for all original information", and, for the gaps, "expert solicitation is a useful way to fill key gaps in your ability to assess uncertainty".

1.2 The GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide (GAO-20-195G, March 2020, https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-20-195G) sets out an estimate as a twelve-step process. Each step leaves documentation behind, and the estimate is judged on four characteristics: comprehensive, well documented, accurate and credible. Each seat's written output, which the next seat starts from, is the same idea applied to a cost-benefit analysis.

1.3 The ISPOR-SMDM Modeling Good Research Practices Task Force sets the academic standard for building a decision model. The requirements conversation and its review follow its report on conceptualising a model (Roberts, Russell, Paltiel, Chambers, McEwan and Krahn 2012, *Medical Decision Making* 32:678–689). That report first states the problem with the decision-makers, then chooses the model's structure to fit the problem, draws on subject experts throughout, and has the model reviewed. On transparency and validation the pipeline follows Eddy et al. 2012 (the same series), as the sheet-twin protocol already does.

1.4 For the published documents and the files behind them, the pipeline follows the American Economic Association's Data and Code Availability Policy and its template README for replication packages (from memory; to be cited from the page): the results come from code anyone can rerun, and the numbers a paper quotes come from those results.

1.5 The AQuA Book (HM Treasury and the Government Analysis Function, 2nd edition, 2025) describes the same life cycle in four stages, each ending in a document the next stage starts from. It sits in the technical appendix's references, not in the text a Washington reader sees first.

1.6 The standards for the second draft's research are in sections 5 and 6.

## 2 One folder and one index per case

2.1 Everything a case uses lives in its folder, `cases/<case>/`: the design, the brief, the inputs, the sources, the model, the results, the elicitation and the documents. Research that serves several cases of one client (an engagement brief, the client's scoping draft) lives in `research/<client>/`, and the case's index links to it.

2.2 `cases/<case>/CASE.md` is the case's index and the first file every stage opens. It holds, in this order: the client, its people and what each document is for and who reads it; the client's own terms, as a glossary every document uses; a pointer to `requirements.md`, the one home of the user's requirements, each in their words with its identifier and their confirmation, and of those still open (`.claude/skills/scope-case/SKILL.md` 6.1; a plan of the analyst's or the referee's stays open there until the user confirms it; a case begun before that file keeps its rulings and open decisions in `CASE.md` until its next conversation records them); and one line each pointing to the technical appendix's limits, which the documents script prints from the record of review points, `review/points.toml` (`cases/README.md`), to `review/`, and to the versions, which `git tag -n '<case>/v*'` lists (2.4.3). It copies no limit, no statement of the review the work has had, no table of versions and no map of files, since each has one home and a copy drifts from it.

2.4 Versions. A case has one folder and one history in git. A version is a state of that history pinned by an annotated git tag, never a copied folder, and the office keeps the versions (`notes/fast_estimate.md` 2.3.3) so the user does nothing by hand (the owner, 2026-10-31: "start doing version control in the case and leave the old one as a reference"; "so I am not doing anything manually, and it is part of the process"). The AQuA Book's record of an analysis includes "change and version control logs" (section 9.3); the numbering follows Semantic Versioning 2.0.0 (semver.org).

2.4.1 A version is numbered v<major>.<minor>, the zero draft v0.0. A major version is a new design: a new structure, new scenarios or branches, a new method for a quantity that moves the result, or a new question. It starts from a new `design.md` and goes through section 3 again, its review included. A minor version is a draft within one design: an input refined, a source added, an error fixed, a layout changed. Each draft that enters version control takes the next minor number, which the typesetter writes in the case's log, `versions.toml`, whose `design` is the major number (2.4.3, 7.1.1; the owner, 2026-11-13: "you can do a naming system that is more human readable. Make shorter case names, and just print an incrementing version number rather than the commit version", with an example of the form `bridge_1page_v2.4`). A change to a case's documents that moves no number (wording, a label, the order of sections, how numbers are rounded or in which unit and period they are shown, layout) is made by the session itself in the template or the case's format (the one exception to the rule that a coordinator changes nothing in a case's work, `notes/fast_estimate.md` 2.3, which holds for anything that moves a number, a method, a source or a claim), the documents are rebuilt at the case's display settings (one `[display]` table, in a fenced ```toml block of its `requirements.md`, or else its `design.md`: `significant_figures`, `unit` and `period`, read by `read_display` and `formats_for` of `cost_benefit_engine/documents.py`; `unit` and `period` record the case's settings for the documents script and the referee, and the compare command checks figures only), and `.venv/bin/python -m cost_benefit_engine.documents compare cases/<case> <folder of the accepted documents' *.md>` checks them: it prints each line of the rebuild that differs from the accepted text by `<file>:<line>`, which the session matches against the change asked, and exits nonzero, naming the number, where a number on a changed line carrying "$", a percentage, a decimal point or a scale word is not a rounding of the same stored value in the case's `results` as the number that stood in its place in the accepted text, has no such number, or shows more figures than the display table allows. No referee follows, since review effort follows what an error would cost; a number typed by hand on an unchanged line, or a bare whole number, is not checked by it. A change that alters a number, a method, a source or a claim is a revision as before.

2.4.2 The tag is `<case>/v<major>.<minor>`, annotated, its message one line saying what the version is. Push it at once with `git push origin <tag>`, since the post-commit hook pushes `main` alone. A session that cannot push a tag, as in Claude Code on the web, does not retry or go round the refusal: it tells the user the version's number and its commit, and that the tag is still to be made from a machine that can push one. Tag a version when its results leave the project (sent to the client, shown in a meeting, published), and before the next major version changes any of its files. A tagged version is never changed: "Once a versioned package has been released, the contents of that version MUST NOT be modified. Any modifications MUST be released as a new version" (Semantic Versioning, item 3). A tag names a number in the log, and is made on a commit that holds that number's block. The typesetter counts the tags `<case>/v<design>.*` as well as the log, so a tagged number is never given to another draft: a case of design 1 tagged v1.0 and v1.1 numbers its next draft v1.2. A case shared with a client or collaborators goes to a repository of its own made from the template by `tools/share_case.py CASE --to OWNER/REPO` (roadmap 2.5), which removes it from the user's repository; its earlier tags stay there, and its new versions are tagged in the shared one.

2.4.3 The tagged versions are listed by `git tag -n '<case>/v*'`, each with its one-line message saying what the version is, and every draft's number is in `versions.toml`; no file keeps a table of them. Each document prints its version: every page carries at its head the notice the build passes, if any, the document's numbered name and the draft's date, joined by " | " ("Draft, preliminary | bridge_1page_v2.4 | 13 November 2026"). The name is the log's `short_name`, the document's short name (`1page` for the summary, `paper`, `appendix` and `note`, with `_verse` after it for a verse edition and `_` and the profile's short name for an audience edition) and the version. The newest number is pending until a commit holds the log with its block: every build with changed inputs keeps it until then, and the first such build after that commit takes the next number, so a number counts the drafts that enter version control and the PDFs of one commit share one. A build whose inputs match a logged draft takes that draft's number and date and writes nothing. A seat commits a draft before it shows it to anyone, so that what is seen has its own number.

2.4.4 New version or update. When a change plainly falls on one side of 2.4.1, the seat decides, and says which in its message to the user. When it does not, or when the change would alter results that have already left the project, the seat asks the user once, as one decision: the change, the two options, what each costs, and its recommendation. The `scope-case` skill asks at its start whenever the request concerns a case that already exists.

2.4.5 The previous version as a reference. Read a file of it with `git show <tag>:<path>`. To run it, check it out into the session's scratchpad with `git worktree add --detach <scratchpad>/<case>-<version> <tag>`, and remove that worktree at the session's close with `git worktree remove`.

2.4.6 A new major version works in the case's own folder and replaces the previous version's files as it goes; the previous version stays whole in its tag. Until that tag exists, it changes no file of the previous version and writes its design to `design_v<major>.md`, renamed to `design.md` once the tag is made. Before its first build of documents it sets `design` in `versions.toml` to the new major number, so that its drafts are numbered from v<major>.0. If another session is still writing the previous version, ask the user whether that session has finished before tagging.

2.4.7 Weighing (CORE 6.2). Caught: a restructure that overwrites the version it should be compared against, and results the client has seen changing without a record; both are at risk whenever two sessions work on one case. Cost: one tag and a push per version, and at most one question to the user per change.

2.5 Language. The user's language governs whatever the user, their experts or their reviewers read; English governs every file the seats read among themselves. `requirements.md` records three lines, `Language, conversation:`, `Language, documents:` and `Language, survey and packets:` (the skill's 0.1.1). Each document is written directly in its recorded language, never translated from a finished English draft; briefs, reviews, editor letters, rulings and the ledger stay in English; a quote in `inputs.toml` is in the source's language, with any English reading in the input's `note` marked as a translation.

## 3 The steps of a case

3.1 The requirements conversation is the `scope-case` skill (`.claude/skills/scope-case/SKILL.md`), which Claude Code starts on its own when a user asks about a new analysis. It ends in a committed `requirements.md`, a draft `CASE.md` and `design.md`, uncommitted, for the author, and the pooled effects and hand estimates; in an exploratory analysis also the committed facts records in the case's `baseline/`, with the options designed with the user committed to `requirements.md` after the research (the skill's section 1).

3.2 The author. The conversation hands its draft, uncommitted, to one author agent, which holds the case from there: it takes the pooled effects and hand estimates the conversation made, or has them made where no conversation ran (`notes/fast_estimate.md` 2.1), and writes them into `estimates.toml` with their stored sources; writes the documents, every number copied from the total tool's output; at the middle tier writes a small model in Python on the toolbox (`cost_benefit_engine/README.md`) for each effect the review names; and it revises them all (`notes/fast_estimate.md` 2). It is reviewed as a journal reviews a paper, at two tiers of rigor below. The owner's four levels (`.claude/skills/scope-case/presets.md` 10.1) are Sketch, Fast, Normal and Full; the card offers Normal as the default, which runs both tiers, fast first (the order is the seat's; the default is the owner's, the owner's message of 2026-11-07: "I am satisfied enough with this to make it the default"), and Full is not offered whole until its systematic review has had its first trial (presets.md 10.2):

3.2.1 The fast tier (`notes/fast_estimate.md`): a draft straight through with no code, a short technical note and a one-page summary that say no review was done, then comments from three blind reviewers and an editor's decision letter on them, with no revision during the run.

3.2.2 The middle tier (`notes/peer_review_trial.md`): the same author models the effects the letter names and revises to milestone 1, the analysis and its technical appendix, answering the letter; the same referees review a second round; an editor rules and checks the fixes; then milestone 2, the policy paper and the summary.

3.2.3 The seats in order, each with its brief in `notes/briefs/`, are listed in `notes/fast_estimate.md` 1.3. A Google Sheet twin, where the user asks for one, is built from the case's estimates and models and a spec by the `sheet-twin` agent (`notes/sheet_twin_protocol.md`), one tab of all the estimates and one tab for each model, after the step the user named and never during a fast run (`.claude/skills/scope-case/presets.md` 10.3, step 1).

## 4 From one draft to the next

4.1 The fast draft's job is to say which effects matter. The measure is each effect's share of the variance of net benefits, which the total tool prints, a covariance allocation of the variance that is not a value of information, and `notes/fast_estimate.md` 2.4 turns it into the rule for which effects get a model. Within a model the same ranking runs over its inputs: the expected value of partial perfect information for each, computed from the draws the run already has (Strong, Oakley and Brennan 2014, *Medical Decision Making* 34:311–326; the ISPOR value-of-information task force, Fenwick et al. 2020, *Value in Health* 23:139–150, and Rothery et al. 2020, 23:277–286), or its share of the variance (its Sobol index).

4.2 The inputs that rank highest are sorted into two kinds: those the literature can answer, and those it cannot. Where both routes exist, both are used, as two independent methods combined by the owner's rule 9 (`notes/owner_cba_rules.md`, the owner's message of 2026-10-31).

4.3 A literature input gets a systematic review sized to its value of information (section 5). An input the literature cannot answer gets a structured expert elicitation (section 6). The elicitation is offered after the Normal draft and not raised before it (the owner, 2026-11-09: "don't bring up the expert elicitations for the fast analysis. leave it as an option for after the medium rigor version"). Both results go to the case's author, which writes them into the model's inputs or the effect's factors with the review or the elicitation record as the source, reruns the model and revises the documents, under the review of its tier (3.2).

4.4 Iteration stops when the expected value of the next round of research is worth less than its cost (the owner's rule 5 of `notes/owner_cba_rules.md`, his post "CBA 301"; the expected value of information is the literature's measure, not his words).

## 5 Systematic review of a literature input

5.1 A literature input's review is run and reported to PRISMA 2020 (Page et al. 2021, *BMJ* 372:n71) by the brief `notes/briefs/systematic_input_review.md`; for an effect size estimated by many economics papers, the MAER-Net reporting guidelines also apply (Havránek et al. 2020, *Journal of Economic Surveys* 34:469–475).

## 6 Expert elicitation

6.1 Standards: the EPA's *Expert Elicitation Task Force White Paper* (August 2011), the US federal account of how an elicitation is planned, piloted, run and documented. Morgan 2014 (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 111:7176–7184) on its use in public policy. Cooke 1991 (*Experts in Uncertainty*), which A-4 cites, for weighting experts by their performance on calibration questions. The IDEA protocol (Hemming et al. 2018, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 9:169–180) for the rounds: each expert estimates alone, sees the others' anonymous answers and reasons, and estimates again.

6.2 One question file per case, `cases/<case>/elicitation/questions.toml`, holds each question. Its questions are the factors of `estimates.toml` and the models' inputs whose source is judgement. Each question has its wording, its channel stated, its units, the factor or input it sets, its anchors from the literature and the four answers IDEA asks for: lowest plausible, highest plausible, best estimate, and how confident the expert is that the truth lies between the two bounds. The same file drives three uses:

6.2.1 A synthetic panel, models answering the same questions with their answers pooled (evidence type `ai_expert_panel`, `cases/README.md`), is the pilot. It tests the wording, and its pooled answers are the stand-ins until real experts answer. A pilot is a step the EPA white paper names.

6.2.2 The Google Form.

6.2.3 The aggregation.

6.3 The form. The harness has no Google Forms connector. The form is therefore made by a short Google Apps Script that a generator writes from `questions.toml`. The user pastes it into a new project at script.google.com and runs it, which grants it the user's own permission to manage forms and no other. It creates the form in the user's account with number-validated answers, every item optional so that an expert skips what lies outside their expertise (the owner, 2026-11-10: "allow anyone to leave things blank if it's not their expertise"), and logs the form's link; answers stay in the form, whose Responses tab links them to a sheet for 6.5. The script is never bound to the case's sheet, since a bound script needs permission over every sheet in the account. A long form takes Apps Script longer than its 6-minute limit per run, so the script stops itself at 4.5 minutes, keeps the form's id in the project's script properties, and finishes the same form when run again. This needs no Google Cloud project, which a newcomer could not be expected to set up. The script is written by `.venv/bin/python tools/elicitation_form.py <questions.toml>`, which writes `form.gs` beside the question file and calls only methods of Google's stored reference (`google_apps_script_forms` in `research/refs.toml`). The Forms API (forms.create and batchUpdate) is the route for a later version. For an expert who reads another language the strings file is prepared by two forward translations reconciled, a back-translation by a second seat checked for meaning, and a pilot with one expert (Wild et al. 2005, ISPOR task force; recalled, not checked against the text).

6.4 Sending. The analyst drafts one email per expert through the Gmail connector. Each email carries a pre-filled link that records the expert's code, not their name. The user approves every send, since an email to an outside person cannot be taken back. A second round repeats this with a feedback page of the first round's anonymous answers.

6.5 Collection to model. A script reads the Responses tab through the Sheets connector. It fits each expert's distribution to their answers in the form the factor or input the question sets already names (`form` in `estimates.toml`, `kind` in `inputs.toml`; `notes/factor_forms.md`), so the question file needs no form of its own and the pooled answer enters the model in that form, pools the experts (equal weights, or Cooke's weights where there are calibration questions) and shows the analyst the pooled result beside each expert's answers. The commonest fault in elicitation data is a unit misread, such as a percentage entered as a fraction, and the analyst checks for it before anything enters the model. On the analyst's approval, the pooled distribution goes to the case's author, which writes it into `inputs.toml` with evidence type `expert_elicitation` and the elicitation record as its source, reruns the model and refills the documents; where the case has a sheet twin, the sheet-twin agent updates its input columns (3.2.3). One approval carries the experts' numbers into both the Python and the sheet.

6.6 Privacy. Experts' names and emails are personal data. They stay in the case folder, never in a public repository or the shared folder. The form asks each expert's consent to be named, and to have their anonymous answers published.

## 7 The machine's publishing tools

7.1 A case's documents are typeset from their Markdown by one command, run from the repository root: `.venv/bin/python tools/typeset.py cases/<case>`. It runs `lualatex` with the `markdown` package and no shell escape, its body in the Type 1 TeX Gyre fonts in T1 encoding (Pagella text, `tgheros` as the sans-serif face) and its title, headings and box titles in Lato, with Tabler icons, both OpenType fonts loaded by `fontspec` from `tools/typeset_layout/assets/` (2026-11-10, roadmap R7), its layout in `tools/typeset_layout/`; `lualatex` needs no shell escape for the `markdown` package where `pdflatex` does. Other LaTeX documents have been built with `pdflatex`, with `pgfplots` for charts and `tcolorbox` for panels. `pdftoppm` renders a page to an image for checking. VS Code's LaTeX Workshop extension runs the same `pdflatex` and previews the PDF as the file is saved. For a document not in English, pass `--language <tag>` before the folder (`ko`, `ja` or `zh`; `tools/typeset_layout/typeset-cjk.sty` is that layout, A4 in Noto CJK; any other tag is refused).

7.1.1 Names and numbers (roadmap item 2.66; `tools/document_names.py`). Before it builds anything, the typesetter reads the case's `versions.toml` (2.4.1 to 2.4.3) and prints two lines: `version v2.4, pending` (or `committed`), and `inputs` with the 16-digit hash of what the build reads, which is each document's Markdown, the images it references, the audience profiles it uses, `tools/typeset.py`, `tools/typeset_layout/`, `tools/document_names.py`, and the `--language` and `--notice` given. It writes each PDF to `documents/drafts/<name>.pdf`, which git ignores, and the same bytes beside its source under the source's name, the path that every reader and every commit list uses; each document's report line ends with its `drafts/` path. It refuses the case in one line on standard error, and builds nothing, when the log is missing or is not TOML, holds a key other than `short_name`, `design` and `[[draft]]`, has no `design` that is a whole number of 0 or more, has a `short_name` that is not lower case, one word or two joined by "-", has a block that lacks a version `vN.M`, a date or `inputs`, or holds one version or one hash twice; and when two documents would share a name. With no `short_name`, it names the documents by the case's folder and prints a line saying so. A committed number's PDF is read from history: `git log --reverse --format=%h -S'version = "v2.4"' -- cases/<case>/versions.toml` gives the commit that first holds its block, and `git show <commit>:cases/<case>/documents/executive_summary.pdf` the PDF.

7.2 LuaLaTeX's font loader (`luaotfload`, in the package `texlive-luatex`) and the Noto CJK fonts (`fonts-noto-cjk`) are what the CJK layout needs (`apt install texlive-luatex fonts-noto-cjk`); the English layout keeps its body in Type 1 fonts. The project's Python environment holds no plotting or PDF library, and adding one changes the lock file. Charts are therefore drawn in `pgfplots`, inside the document.

7.3 Chart colours come from the validated palette of the `dataviz` skill (light mode): blue `#2A78D6` for the result a figure is about; orange `#EB6834` and violet `#6250D6` for a pair such as low and high; warm grey `#C3C2B7` for context.

## 8 The weighing (`standards/process/controls.md`)

8.1 The case for it: without one index per case, a seat spends many tool calls finding what it needs before it writes a word.

8.2 The faults it removes: numbers typed by hand into a document, with no check against their source, are left stale when another session reruns the model.

8.3 Its cost: a few lines of `CASE.md` per stage; one plain-language name per input; one line of the sheet-twin protocol; the facts script and the form generator, each built through `build` at standard risk; and the document templates, made once. The referee's review at each stage and the reviews and elicitations of section 4 are the analysis itself, not overhead, and run only where an input's value of information pays for them.

8.4 Public release. The harness reads rules from the owner's shared folder (`shared/`), and a public copy carries the files its stages read: the referee's definition, the modelling red flags, the house writing rules and the controls method.
