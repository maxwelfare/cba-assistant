# The documents of a case: what the literature asks of them

Research note of 2026-11-05, written by a research seat of the workflow that designs stage 5 of the case pipeline, the documents seat (`notes/case_pipeline.md` 3.6, 3.7 and section 9). It answers the owner's ruling of the same day: "1.2.3 make the seat first. The focus is on making the process right, not doing this particular analysis. Make the full and proper process, no shortcuts, it could take all night and thats fine. Only after its one run the test." The three documents are his: "the plain-language 1-pager executive summary, then a 20ish page policy paper aimed at educated nonspecialist audiences (the RFF paper on energy investment is a good model), and then the technical appendix written like an academic paper with all the citations and formulas to satisfy real scrutiny" (the owner's message of 2026-10-31).

Method. The libraries were searched first (`tools.library search`, several dozen queries); they held the Circular A-4 texts, CHEERS 2022, the Green Book 2026, the Office of Impact Analysis note of 2023, the Reference Case guidelines, Boardman et al. 2018, van der Bles et al. 2019 and 2020, the AEA's data and code policy, Panko 2000, Herndon et al. 2014, Bisbee et al. 2024, and in the shared library the AQuA Book and Fagan 1976. The rest came from the web. Each source says how it was read: "opened" means its text was fetched in this session (by `curl` and `pdftotext`, or a fetch) and every quotation below was matched to that text; "abstract" means only the abstract was read (PubMed or Crossref); "recalled" means from memory, with at most the bibliographic record confirmed. The downloads sit in the session's scratchpad, not in the repository: this is a research note, and a case that relies on one of them stores it. Each source has its entry in `research/refs.toml`.

## 1 What the stage must make

1.1 A case ends in three documents: a one-page summary in plain language, a policy paper of about 20 pages for educated non-specialists, and a technical appendix written as an academic paper (`notes/what_a_case_delivers.md`, items 1 to 3). The one-page summary is in every preset; the policy paper and the appendix are optional steps 23 and 24 of the menu, on in the Draft for review and Full analysis presets and off in the Quick estimate.

1.2 The literature below is read for every preset and every kind of case (formulaic, speculative, a pure benefit estimate, a full net-benefit analysis) (CORE rule 6; the shared memory `dont-reify-an-example`).

## 2 Reporting standards for cost-benefit analyses

2.1 **OMB Circular A-4 (2003).** Office of Management and Budget (17 September 2003), *Circular A-4: Regulatory Analysis*, reinstated by Memorandum M-25-15 of 12 February 2025. Opened: the stored text, the one now in `research/sources/omb_2003_circulara4/text.txt`, split into sentences and searched.

2.1.1 What it requires of a document. "A good analysis is transparent. It should be possible for a qualified third party reading the report to see clearly how you arrived at your estimates and conclusions." "A good analysis provides specific references to all sources of data, appendices with documentation of models (where necessary), and the results of formal sensitivity and other uncertainty analyses. Your analysis should also have an executive summary, including a standardized accounting statement." The accounting statement reports benefits and costs in three categories, monetized, quantified but not monetized, and qualitative, which "are mutually exclusive and exhaustive" (the stored text drops a semicolon in the list, so it is paraphrased here); it presents "undiscounted streams of benefit and cost estimates (monetized and net) for each year", reports transfers separately, and gives "expected-value estimates as well as distributions", using "the 95 and 5 percent confidence bounds" when only bounds are given. The unquantified effects go in "a summary table that lists all the unquantified benefits and costs", ranked by importance; where they matter, a "threshold" analysis asks how large they would have to be to change the sign of net benefits. Incremental as well as total benefits and costs are shown.

2.1.2 Refinements. A rule of precision: "Your estimates cannot be more precise than their most uncertain component. Thus, your analysis should report estimates in a way that reflects the degree of uncertainty and not create a false sense of precision"; "an estimate of $220 million implies rounding to the nearest $10 million". The presentation "should also explain how your analytical choices have affected your results", and "Worst-case or conservative analyses are not usually adequate because they do not convey the complete probability distribution of outcomes". Its depth is tiered by the rule's size (a formal probabilistic analysis is required above $1 billion a year), which is a proportionality rule.

2.2 **OMB Circular A-94 (1992), reinstated in 2025.** Office of Management and Budget (29 October 1992), *Circular A-94: Guidelines and Discount Rates for Benefit-Cost Analysis of Federal Programs*; the 2023 revision was revoked and the 1992 text reinstated by Vought RT, Memorandum M-25-23, *Rescission and Reinstatement of Circular No. A-94* (8 April 2025). Opened: the memorandum (PDF from whitehouse.gov) and the 1992 text (the archived White House page); the 2023 text was also fetched but is not in force.

2.2.1 What it requires. "The analysis should include a statement of the assumptions, the rationale behind them, and a review of their strengths and weaknesses. Key data and results, such as year-by-year estimates of benefits and costs, should be reported to promote independent analysis and review." "Ideally, probability distributions of potential benefits, costs, and net benefits should be presented", and "the basis for the probability distribution assumptions should be reported". Net present value is "the standard criterion", and effects outside the United States "should be reported separately".

2.2.2 Refinement. A-94 names the year-by-year table as the reader's means of checking the analysis independently; it is the document-side twin of the owner's rule 7 (discount year by year where it can be seen).

2.3 **CHEERS 2022.** Husereau D, Drummond M, Augustovski F, et al. (2022), Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) statement: updated reporting guidance for health economic evaluations, *BMJ* 376:e067975, doi:10.1136/bmj-2021-067975 (published jointly in several journals). Opened: the PMC full text, table 1 read item by item.

2.3.1 What it requires. A 28-item checklist in seven parts: title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, other information. The items a cost-benefit document must meet include: a structured abstract "that highlights context, key methods, results, and alternative analyses" (item 2); the comparators, perspective, time horizon and discount rate, each "and why chosen" (7 to 10); the price date and currency (15); the model, and "if the model is publicly available and where it can be accessed" (16); distributional effects (19); "Report all analytic inputs (such as values, ranges, references) including uncertainty or distributional assumptions" (22); "Report the mean values for the main categories of costs and outcomes of interest" (23); "Describe how uncertainty about analytic judgments, inputs, or projections affect findings" (24); limitations and "ethical or equity considerations not captured" (26); funding and "any role of the funder in the identification, design, conduct, and reporting of the analysis" (27); conflicts of interest (28).

2.3.2 Refinements. Box 1: the 2022 language was "broadened to make CHEERS more widely applicable to cost-benefit/benefit-cost analysis", so it is the nearest thing to a journal reporting standard for the appendix. Users "indicate the section of the manuscript where relevant information can be found", and an item that does not apply is reported as "Not applicable", never left silent; that is the form a preset's omitted step takes in a document. It is a standard of reporting, not of conduct, and has no validated score.

2.4 **HM Treasury Green Book (2026).** HM Treasury (2026), *The Green Book: UK Government Guidance on Appraisal*, revised 5 February 2026. Opened: the PDF, chapter 9 ("Presentation of results") and paragraphs 2.30 to 2.32 and 6.69 to 6.76.

2.4.1 What it requires. "Practitioners should therefore present appraisal results clearly and transparently, showing the value for money of alternative options in a consistent way. They should also explain why the preferred option has been recommended over other potential options" (9.1). A business case "should begin with an executive summary" (9.2). Results are presented in an appraisal summary table, one row per shortlisted option (business as usual, do minimum, preferred, more and less ambitious) and five columns: the option, the summary metrics of social value, the unmonetisable costs and benefits, the public sector financial impact, and the distribution among groups and places, each with "Expected values and key uncertainties" (9.3 to 9.6, table 12).

2.4.2 Refinements. The table "helps to avoid the tendency to reduce appraisal results to a simple comparison of benefit-cost ratios" (9.3). Uncertainty is shown in every column, "using descriptive text, approximate ranges, or confidence intervals, depending on what is most proportionate" (9.5). An interval is read as a probability statement: a 90 per cent interval of [100, 140] "means that there is a 90% chance" the value lies in it (6.70). "Practitioners should consider tail risks proportionately", since intervals and P90 values "often do not adequately capture these risks" (6.72); and switching values, already used by the project, are a presentation device as well as an analysis (the existing entry in `research/refs.toml`).

2.5 **Australia's Office of Impact Analysis (2026).** Office of Impact Analysis, Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (2026), *Australian Government Impact Analysis Framework: Practical Guide for Agencies* (25 pp., PDF of June 2026), and *The 7 Impact Analysis questions* (2023). Opened: both PDFs. The framework document itself (July 2026) returned a bot page and was not read.

2.5.1 What it requires. Every impact analysis answers seven questions: the problem and the data, the objectives and why government must act, the options, "What is the likely net benefit of each option?", whom the agency consulted, the best option, and how it will be evaluated. Its document comes in two layers: "Where a Detailed IA is required to provide in-depth analysis to support policy development, agencies will still need to prepare a Dashboard IA as the executive summary." Before the decision, "Agencies must endorse a Statement of Sufficiency", signed by a Deputy Secretary or higher and published with the analysis; a self-assessment rubric checks each question "has been answered in an appropriate level of detail".

2.5.2 Refinements. A named person attests that the document is sufficient for the decision, and the attestation is published with it; and the short layer is a required part of every analysis, not an optional digest. The Office's cost-benefit note of 2023 (in `research/refs.toml`) adds that hard-to-monetise impacts do not invalidate the method.

2.6 **The Reference Case guidelines (2019).** Robinson LA, Hammitt JK, Cecchini M, et al. (2019), *Reference Case Guidelines for Benefit-Cost Analysis in Global Health and Development: Summary and Recommendations*. Opened: section 7, "Summarizing and Presenting the Results".

2.6.1 What it requires. "The documentation must describe the problem the policy is designed to address, the options considered, the analytic approach, and the results, as well as the implications of uncertainties", and it "should be written so that members of the lay public can understand the analysis and conclusions. It should also provide enough detail for expert review; ideally, competent analysts should be able to reconstruct the analysis or at minimum explore the implications of changing key assumptions."

2.6.2 Refinements. "Regardless of whether a benefit-cost ratio or IRR is featured, it is generally valuable to also report estimates of net benefits to indicate the magnitude of the welfare gains, along with information on the distribution of the impacts"; costs are what implementing the policy uses and benefits are its consequences, and an offsetting effect goes in the category of the effect it offsets, because a ratio depends on that classification.

2.7 **Boardman et al. (2018).** Boardman AE, Greenberg DH, Vining AR, Weimer DL (2018), *Cost-Benefit Analysis: Concepts and Practice*, 5th ed., Cambridge University Press. Recalled, not opened (paywalled): the textbook's nine steps end with a recommendation based on net present value and the sensitivity analysis, and it treats the analysis as a decision aid whose report states the alternatives, standing, impacts, monetization, discounting and sensitivity in that order. No page is cited.

2.8 Where the standards agree, and where they differ.

2.8.1 They agree that every cost-benefit document states: the question and the decision it serves; the options and the baseline they are measured against; whose costs and benefits count; the time horizon, discount rates and price year; the headline as an expected value with its uncertainty; the split of benefits and costs by kind and by who bears them, with transfers apart; what was not quantified, ranked; the limits; and every source (A-4; A-94; CHEERS items 7 to 15, 19, 22 to 26; Green Book chapter 9; Office of Impact Analysis questions 1 to 7; Reference Case section 7).

2.8.2 They agree on two layers at least: a short summary that stands alone in front of a full report (A-4's executive summary with the accounting statement; the Green Book's executive summary; the Dashboard IA; CHEERS's structured abstract).

2.8.3 They differ on the interval: A-4 asks for the 5th and 95th percentiles; the Green Book's example is a 90 per cent interval; RFF reports the 10th, 50th and 90th (section 3). The engine's `run.json` holds the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles and the mean (`notes/case_pipeline.md` 3.4), which meets A-4 and the Green Book.

2.8.4 They differ on the lead measure: A-94 and the Reference Case lead with net present value and the Green Book warns against a contest of ratios, while RFF's headline is a ratio (3.4.1). The project's key result is net benefits (the project memory `key-result-is-net-benefits`).

## 3 The policy paper's model: RFF Working Paper 21-10

3.1 Identification. Shawhan D, Cleary K, Funke C, Witkin S (2021), *The Value of Advanced Energy Funding: Projected Effects of Proposed US Funding for Advanced Energy Technologies*, Working Paper 21-10, Resources for the Future, Washington, April 2021. Opened: the PDF at `media.rff.org`, the link the owner emailed to a client on 2026-10-28 as the example of the deliverable, which he called the minimum viable product (his engagement brief for that client). Its companion is Shawhan D, Cleary K, Funke C, Witkin S (13 August 2021), *The Value of Advanced Energy Funding: Analysis for Active Legislative Discussion*, RFF Issue Brief; opened by a fetch that summarized it (about 1,400 words; sections Context, Summary of Study, Findings, Conclusions; four figures). The owner's "RFF paper on energy investment" is the working paper.

3.2 Structure. 38 pages. Front matter: about the authors, acknowledgments that name every participating expert by technology, and about RFF. An executive summary of two pages (about 850 words) with one figure, ES1, the benefit-cost ratio of each technology with and without a national clean electricity standard. Contents, then a body of 25 pages (about 9,000 words, 12 figures, 4 tables): 1 Introduction; 2 Methods for Conducting the Expert Elicitation; 3 Results, in four subsections (effects on spending, on technology costs, the benefits, and economy-wide benefits for one technology); 4 Discussion; 5 Conclusion; 6 References, about two dozen, grouped by topic. Appendices A (cost assumptions used to harmonize the experts' answers) and B (projected competitiveness), and an online appendix with the questionnaires, instructions and bill summaries the experts saw.

3.3 Style. First person plural, short declarative sentences, every acronym defined at first use, methods explained in words with no equation in the body (the weighted least-squares fit of a skew-normal distribution to the experts' percentiles is described in prose). The results are translated to one household: "These electricity user savings amount to an average of approximately $14 per household per year for each technology without a CES and $56 per household per year for each technology with a CES." What is not counted is said in the summary itself: "These benefit-to-cost ratios do not count benefits outside the years 2040 to 2060, US RD&D expenditure changes outside the 10-year period from 2022 to 2031, US export revenues, US benefits from reduced foreign emissions, or net benefits abroad." The Discussion lists five caveats in turn: the omitted benefits; "a large amount of unavoidable uncertainty around the central estimates"; the experts' possible bias, in both directions, with the evidence for each; a conservative assumption, named as such; and a non-random set of experts. How the experts were found, paid ($1,000 each) and kept anonymous is stated.

3.4 Uncertainty. Each expert gave the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of cost; box plots show the 5th to 95th percentiles of the fitted distributions (Figure 8); the benefit-cost ratios are shown as trios of bars at the three cost percentiles (Figures 9, 10); and an approximate expected value takes "weights of 30 percent on the 10th percentile and 90th percentile values and 40 percent on the 50th percentile value" (Figure 11), which is Swanson's 30-40-30 rule (Hurst, Brown and Swanson 2000, AAPG Bulletin 84(12):1883-1891; record confirmed in Crossref, content recalled). Table 2 tests the benefit lifetime at 10, 20 and 30 years, and shows benefits and costs separately in 2020 dollars at a 3 per cent real rate.

3.4.1 The headline figure, ES1, shows the ratios at central values only, with no interval, and the headline measure is the benefit-cost ratio ("an average benefit-to-cost ratio for each technology of 6.9 without the CES and 10.5 with it").

3.5 What the policy paper takes from it, and where it departs.

3.5.1 It takes the sequence (a two-page summary with one headline figure; introduction; methods in words; results by stream; a discussion that lists what is omitted and every known bias; a short conclusion; references; appendices for the technical detail and the instruments), the translation to one household or person, the plain register with no equation in the body, and the naming of the experts and how they were recruited.

3.5.2 It departs in four places, each on a source above. It leads with expected net benefits and shows the ratio second (A-94; Reference Case 7(b)(ii); Green Book 9.3; the project memory `key-result-is-net-benefits`). Its headline figure carries the interval (the owner's rule 1; A-4). It reports the mean of the Monte Carlo draws, never a 30-40-30 approximation, since the draws exist (A-4: "expected-value estimates as well as distributions"). It reports every discount rate the case's guideline names, not one.

3.5.3 The working paper is written for a semi-technical reader, and RFF's own version for non-specialists is the 1,400-word issue brief. The owner's 20-page paper for educated non-specialists sits between them: the working paper's structure and length in a plainer register, which is the Canadian rule of 4.1: "don't lapse into academic style just because you have more room."

## 4 Summaries and policy papers for non-specialists

4.1 **The 1:3:25 rule.** Canadian Health Services Research Foundation (undated; commonly dated 2001, recalled), *Reader-Friendly Writing - 1:3:25*, Communication Notes, Ottawa. Opened: the two-page copy hosted by Fraser Health; an archived copy of 2006 exists in the Library and Archives Canada web archive (search result, not opened).

4.1.1 What it requires. "start with one page of main messages; follow that with a three-page executive summary; present your findings in no more than 25 pages of writing, in language a bright, educated, but not research-trained person would understand." The main messages are not a summary of findings: "you have to go one step further and tell your audience what you think the findings mean for them." The executive summary "is not an academic abstract; it's much more like a newspaper story, where the most interesting stuff goes at the top", with no "more than a line or two about your approach, methods and other technical details". The report covers seven parts in order: context, implications, approach, results, additional resources, further research, references; "Highly technical material can be an appendix", and "Results that don't relate directly to the conclusions should be moved to an appendix." Before release, "take the time to show it to your decision-maker partners."

4.1.2 Refinements. This is the established form of the owner's three documents, and it brings two refinements. The one page carries implications for the reader's decision, not a digest of results; and implications come before approach in the long report. Where research cannot yet support a recommendation, "don't just fall back on 'more research is needed.' Use your main messages to define the questions that still need to be asked", which in a cost-benefit analysis is what the expected value of information names (the owner's rule 5).

4.2 **The Federal Plain Language Guidelines.** Plain Language Action and Information Network (2011), *Federal Plain Language Guidelines*, March 2011, revision 1 May 2011 (edition data recalled); now kept by the General Services Administration as Digital.gov's *Plain language guide series*. Opened: the Digital.gov pages on principles, writing, design and testing, and, in GSA's GitHub repository for plainlanguage.gov, the archived guideline pages "Organize the information", "Place the main idea before exceptions and conditions" and "Paraphrase testing" (their redirect lines show they are the 2011 guideline pages). The Plain Writing Act of 2010 (Public Law 111-274) requires federal agencies to write their documents for the public in plain language, and the guidelines are the government's reference for meeting it (recalled).

4.2.1 What it requires. "The first rule of plain language is: write for your audience", which is not to "dumb down" the content. "Start by stating your purpose and the bottom line. Lay things out in a logical order. Put the most important information at the beginning and include background information (when necessary) toward the end." "Start with your main idea – not an exception." Use the active voice, the present tense, and verbs rather than nouns made from them; short sections; useful headings; and "Create logically structured pages with key information at the top."

4.2.2 Refinements. The guidelines include their own test (7.3.1), and their own limit on it: testing "probably isn't worth the time to test documents that go to a small number of people", a proportionality rule.

4.3 **Policy papers.** Young E, Quinn L (2002), *Writing Effective Public Policy Papers: A Guide for Policy Advisers in Central and Eastern Europe*, Local Government and Public Service Reform Initiative, Open Society Institute, Budapest. Existence confirmed in search results (NCCMT registry record); the PDF copies tried returned web pages, so the content is recalled: a policy study runs title, contents, executive summary, introduction, the problem, the options, conclusions and recommendations, appendices and references, and argues from the problem to a recommendation rather than reporting research in the order it was done. Minto B, *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* (first published 1978; edition not fixed), recalled: the answer first, then the arguments that support it, grouped, which is the business-writing form of the plain-language "bottom line first".

4.4 **Charts and tables.** Urban Institute, *Data Visualization Style Guide* (urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide). Opened. "Urban charts require a title, source line, and axis or other labels identifying the elements and units of the chart"; "Use active, concise titles", titles "that tell the reader what they are supposed to learn and take away from the graph"; "When possible, avoid using legends and instead directly label the data on the graph." Its type floors (8.5 pt labels, 8 pt notes) are already the standard of `notes/pdf_layout_review.md` 2.2. Schwabish JA (2014), An economist's guide to visualizing data, *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 28(1):209-234, and Schwabish J (2020), Ten guidelines for better tables, *Journal of Benefit-Cost Analysis* 11(2):151-178: records confirmed in Crossref; the 2014 paper is recalled (show the data, reduce clutter, integrate text and graph), and the 2020 guidelines are applied in `notes/sheet_layout_review.md` 3.1.

4.5 **Quality and uncertainty in a release.** Government Analysis Function (UK) (17 December 2018), *Communicating quality, uncertainty and change*. Opened.

4.5.1 What it requires. Layers by reader: some users are "expert analysts" and "technical users" while "inquiring citizens" are not, and "We should offer users the opportunity to browse through the information according to their needs". "Vital messages about quality – those that have a profound impact on what can be drawn from the numbers – should be presented up front", and "Critical quality caveats or warnings should be included in the commentary besides the points that they relate to." Its model headline puts the range first: "between 220 and 250 people were killed ... with a central estimate of 230 deaths." Analysts "explain what you can and what you cannot conclude from the findings", and "be prepared to use different terms for different audiences—the terms 'error' and 'bias' could be misinterpreted by a non-statistical audience".

4.5.2 Refinement. A revisions policy: corrections after publication are announced and explained, so a document carries its version and a note of what changed since the last.

## 5 Communicating intervals and uncertainty

5.1 **The framework.** van der Bles AM, van der Linden S, Freeman ALJ, Mitchell J, Galvao AB, Zaval L, Spiegelhalter DJ (2019), Communicating uncertainty about facts, numbers and science, *Royal Society Open Science* 6(5):181870. Opened: the PubMed record (which confirms the author list) and the PMC full text, sections 4.1 and the closing advice.

5.1.1 What it requires. A communicator asks what is uncertain (a fact, a number, a hypothesis), why (variation, measurement, limited knowledge, disagreement), and separates direct uncertainty (about the quantity) from indirect uncertainty (the quality of the evidence behind it). It orders nine expressions of direct uncertainty by their precision, from a full probability distribution down to not mentioning uncertainty and denying it. "Most importantly, keep your expressions of the magnitude of uncertainty clearly separate from the magnitude of any evidence you are trying to communicate", and "if you can test the effect of your communication with your audience at all, then do".

5.1.2 Refinement. Its sequel found that stating uncertainty numerically cost "only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication" (van der Bles et al. 2020, *PNAS* 117(14):7672-7683; abstract, in `research/refs.toml`). A number with its range is safer for trust than a verbal hedge.

5.2 **The IPCC's guidance.** Mastrandrea MD, Field CB, Stocker TF, et al. (2010), *Guidance Note for Lead Authors of the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report on Consistent Treatment of Uncertainties*, IPCC. Opened.

5.2.1 What it requires. Two metrics: confidence in a finding, from the "type, amount, quality, and consistency of evidence" and "the degree of agreement", expressed qualitatively; and likelihood, from a calibrated scale: virtually certain 99 to 100 per cent, very likely 90 to 100, likely 66 to 100, about as likely as not 33 to 66, unlikely 0 to 33, very unlikely 0 to 10, exceptionally unlikely 0 to 1. Each key finding carries "a traceable account" of the evidence and agreement behind it.

5.2.2 Refinements. Framing: "a 10% chance of dying is interpreted more negatively than a 90% chance of surviving", so "Consider reciprocal statements". Against group overconfidence: "ask each member of the author team to write down his or her individual assessments of the level of uncertainty before entering into a group discussion."

5.3 **How readers take the calibrated words.** Budescu DV, Broomell S, Por H-H (2009), Improving communication of uncertainty in the reports of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, *Psychological Science* 20(3):299-308. Abstract: readers' numerical readings of the IPCC's terms "deviated significantly from the IPCC guidelines, even when the respondents had access to these guidelines". Budescu DV, Por H-H, Broomell SB, Smithson M (2014), The interpretation of IPCC probabilistic statements around the world, *Nature Climate Change* 4(6):508-512: record confirmed; content recalled, that the same misreading holds across many countries and languages and that putting the numerical range beside the word ("very likely, 90 to 100 per cent") narrows it. The refinement: a calibrated word is never printed without its number.

5.4 **How readers take a range.** Dieckmann NF, Peters E, Gregory R (2015), At home on the range? Lay interpretations of numerical uncertainty ranges, *Risk Analysis* 35(7):1281-1295. Abstract: "substantial variation exists in how people perceive the distribution underlying numerical ranges"; less numerate readers are less likely to read it as roughly normal; "including a best estimate increases perceptions that the distribution is roughly normal"; and "simple graphical representations can decrease the variance in distributional perceptions". The refinement: a range is printed with its central estimate, and in the policy paper with a small picture of the distribution.

5.5 **Pictures of distributions.** Spiegelhalter D, Pearson M, Short I (2011), Visualizing uncertainty about the future, *Science* 333(6048):1393-1400. Abstract: the evidence on how visualizations are understood is limited, effectiveness "clearly depends on the relative numeracy of an audience", and "communicating deeper uncertainties resulting from incomplete or disputed knowledge ... remains a challenge". Hullman J, Resnick P, Adar E (2015), Hypothetical outcome plots outperform error bars and violin plots for inferences about reliability of variable ordering, *PLOS ONE* 10(11):e0142444. Abstract: showing a set of individual draws one after another let people judge "two and three quantities" much more accurately than error bars or violin plots. Fernandes M, Walls L, Munson S, Hullman J, Kay M (2018), Uncertainty displays using quantile dotplots or CDFs improve transit decision-making, *Proceedings of CHI 2018*, 1-12: record confirmed, abstract closed; recalled, that a quantile dotplot (a fixed number of dots, each an equal share of probability) and a cumulative curve led to better decisions than intervals or densities. The refinement: a Monte Carlo model's own draws are the material for its pictures; in a static PDF the quantile dotplot is the form that survives printing (an animated plot does not).

5.6 **Frequencies.** Gigerenzer G, Hoffrage U (1995), How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: frequency formats, *Psychological Review* 102(4):684-704. Record confirmed; recalled: people reason better from natural frequencies ("in 9 of 10 simulated futures") than from single-event probabilities. The plain-language summary can state the chance that net benefits are positive this way.

5.7 **Reviews of the field.** Spiegelhalter D (2017), Risk and uncertainty communication, *Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application* 4:31-60. Abstract: "the primary message is to acknowledge expert guidance, be clear about objectives, and work closely with intended audiences." Fischhoff B, Davis AL (2014), Communicating scientific uncertainty, *PNAS* 111(Suppl 4):13664-13671. Abstract: what to communicate "depends on the decisions that people face": a signal, a choice "among fixed options", or "learning to create options". A cost-benefit analysis serves the second, so the summary tells the reader how likely the uncertainty is to change the choice, not only how wide it is.

5.8 **Incredible certitude.** Manski CF (2019), Communicating uncertainty in policy analysis, *PNAS* 116(16):7634-7641. Abstract: "A prevalent practice has been to report policy analysis with incredible certitude. That is, exact predictions of policy outcomes are routine, while expressions of uncertainty are rare." Recalled: the paper's examples include official budget scores given as single numbers, and it asks for interval estimates with the assumptions behind them stated.

5.9 What these sources ask, together. The headline is the mean with its 90 per cent interval in numbers, and in the summary the chance that net benefits have the sign stated, in plain words with the number beside them (A-4; Green Book 6.70; Mastrandrea et al. 2010; Budescu et al. 2009; Gigerenzer and Hoffrage 1995). The quality of the evidence is said separately from the size of the range (van der Bles et al. 2019; the IPCC's confidence). A range always carries its central estimate, and figures are drawn from the draws (Dieckmann et al. 2015; Hullman et al. 2015; Fernandes et al. 2018). Numbers are rounded to the precision of the most uncertain input (A-4). The reader is told whether the uncertainty could change the choice (Fischhoff and Davis 2014; the Green Book's switching values). This seat's inference, not a source's words: since the interval is a percentile range of a simulated distribution, the appendix names it as such ("the 5th to 95th percentile of the simulated net benefits") rather than as a confidence interval, which a statistician reads as a frequentist statement.

## 6 Documents whose numbers come from the model

6.1 **Literate programming and dynamic documents.** Knuth DE (1984), Literate programming, *The Computer Journal* 27(2):97-111, record confirmed; recalled: a program and its explanation are written as one source from which both the code and the document are made. Leisch F (2002), Sweave: dynamic generation of statistical reports using literate data analysis, in *Compstat 2002: Proceedings in Computational Statistics*, Physica-Verlag, 575-580, recalled; and Xie Y (2015), *Dynamic Documents with R and knitr*, 2nd ed., Chapman and Hall/CRC, recalled: the report's numbers, tables and figures are computed when the document is built, so a change of data or code changes the report and nothing is copied by hand.

6.2 **Connecting words to results.** Sandve GK, Nekrutenko A, Taylor J, Hovig E (2013), Ten simple rules for reproducible computational research, *PLoS Computational Biology* 9(10):e1003285. Opened. Rule 1, "For Every Result, Keep Track of How It Was Produced"; rule 2, "Avoid Manual Data Manipulation Steps"; rule 7, "Always Store Raw Data behind Plots"; rule 9, "Connect Textual Statements to Underlying Results": "we suggest that statements are connected to underlying results already from the time the statements are initially formulated", by "a simple file path to detailed results, or the ID of a result in an analysis framework, included within the text itself". The refinement is that rule 9 covers statements, not only numbers: an interpretation ("the benefits exceed the costs") is tied to the result that supports it.

6.3 **One script, everything automated.** Gentzkow M, Shapiro JM (2014), *Code and Data for the Social Sciences: A Practitioner's Guide*, University of Chicago mimeo. Opened. Rules "(A) Automate everything that can be automated. (B) Write a single script that executes all code from beginning to end." Its example of what goes wrong is a directory where a reader cannot tell "Which numbers in the log files correspond to the numbers reported in the paper?", or whether the submitted PDF is the built one.

6.4 **Reproducible analytical pipelines.** Government Analysis Function (UK) (20 June 2022), *Reproducible Analytical Pipelines (RAP) strategy*. Opened. At a minimum a pipeline must "minimise manual steps, for example copy-paste, point-click, or drag-drop operation", must "deepen technical and quality assurance processes with peer review", must "guarantee an audit trail using version control software", and must document any manual step that remains. The AQuA Book makes this the "recommended approach for developing analysis in code" (its chapter 6; 7.1 below).

6.5 **Replication packages.** American Economic Association, *Data and Code Availability Policy* and its Data and Code Availability Standard: in `research/refs.toml` (fetched 2026-11-02), its README must give "Information mapping programs to output and how each output relates to the exhibits in the paper and appendices". Peng RD (2011), Reproducible research in computational science, *Science* 334(6060):1226-1227, record confirmed; recalled: reproducibility as a spectrum from publication alone to code, data and the means to rerun them. Vilhuber L (2020), Reproducibility and replicability in economics, *Harvard Data Science Review* 2(4), record confirmed, page refused the fetch; recalled: the AEA's data editor reruns each accepted paper's package and compares its output with the tables, figures and numbers in the manuscript before publication.

6.6 **How such documents are checked.** Nuijten MB, Hartgerink CHJ, van Assen MALM, Epskamp S, Wicherts JM (2016), The prevalence of statistical reporting errors in psychology (1985-2013), *Behavior Research Methods* 48(4):1205-1226. Abstract: "half of all published psychology papers that use NHST contained at least one p-value that was inconsistent with its test statistic and degrees of freedom. One in eight papers contained a grossly inconsistent p-value"; it proposes a "co-pilot model" of a second author checking results, and running the checker on one's own manuscript. The refinement is the check's form: recompute a reported number from the other reported numbers it depends on, and flag a mismatch. The cost of a number copied by hand is recorded in the project's library: one inspector finds about half of a spreadsheet's errors (Panko 2000), and a published result's spreadsheet error was found only by outside replicators (Herndon, Ash and Pollin 2014; the detail recalled, as its entry says). Hunt A, Thomas D (1999), *The Pragmatic Programmer*, Addison-Wesley, recalled: "Every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation within a system", which is what `facts.json` is for the documents.

6.7 What these sources ask, together. Every number, table and figure in a document is made from the model's output when the document is built, through one script, from a clean checkout, and the built document records the commit that made it (Knuth 1984; Gentzkow and Shapiro 2014; Sandve et al. 2013 rules 1 and 2; RAP 2022). Each number points to the key of the result it shows (Sandve et al. 2013 rule 9; AEA). Two checks follow from the literature. The first, which the pipeline already names (`notes/case_pipeline.md` 3.7), refuses a number typed into the text. The second it does not yet name: words that depend on a number ("positive", "more than doubles", "likely", "the largest stream") are themselves results, and a rebuilt number can contradict a typed word; so each such word is generated from the facts or checked against them, the statcheck form applied to prose (Sandve et al. 2013 rule 9; Nuijten et al. 2016). A round trip, numbers read back from the rendered PDF and matched to the facts, is the form of the AEA data editor's comparison (Vilhuber 2020, recalled).

## 7 How analytical reports are reviewed before release

7.1 **The AQuA Book (2025).** Government Operational Research Service, Government Analysis Function, Government Actuary's Department and Office for National Statistics (30 July 2025), *The AQuA Book: guidance on producing quality analysis for government*, revised edition, GOV.UK. Opened: the single GOV.UK page, chapters 2, 3, 5, 6, 8 and 9 read.

7.1.1 What it requires. Four roles: commissioner, analyst, assurer, approver. "The assurer and the analyst shall be independent", the degree of separation depending on the importance and complexity of the work (chapter 3), and "The roles of analyst and assurer shall be distinct from each other" (5.1). Assurance is proportionate to risk, judged as the effect of an error times its likelihood (3.2); "for complex analysis ... options such as external peer review may be necessary", and under time constraints, "addressing the areas of greatest risk should take priority. You should inform the commissioner about how you have addressed the risks and any remaining risks together with appropriate caveats." At delivery, "The assurer should communicate the assurance state to the approver", confirming the work was "appropriately scoped, executed, validated, verified, documented" and "provides adequate handling of uncertainty" (9.1.3). The delivered documentation includes "advice on uncertainty and its affect on the outputs", "a description of the limits of the analysis and what it can and cannot be used for", "the test plan and results of the tests" and "a statement of assurance" (9.3). "The communication should be quality assured in a proportionate manner to ensure an accurate reflection of the analytical results" (9.2.2).

7.1.2 Refinements. Validation, as distinct from verification, "is testing whether the product meets the requirements of users. It is important to involve the users in the process" (8.2.1); and "The analyst may consider testing communication with different user groups to ensure that the analytical outputs are used as intended" (9.6). The documents carry their assurance state.

7.2 **Peer review of influential analysis.** Office of Management and Budget (2004), *Final Information Quality Bulletin for Peer Review*, issued by Memorandum M-05-03 (16 December 2004). Opened.

7.2.1 What it requires. For a highly influential assessment the agency publishes "the written charge to the peer reviewers, the peer reviewers' names, the peer reviewers' report(s), and the agency's response". Reviewers have the needed expertise, and their "potential conflicts of interest ... and independence from the agency" are addressed. "The charge to the reviewers should be determined in advance of the selection of the" reviewers; "The charge should ask that peer reviewers ensure that scientific uncertainties are clearly identified and characterized", and "that the potential implications of the uncertainties for the technical conclusions drawn are clear"; but "the charge should make clear that the reviewers are not to provide advice on the policy".

7.2.2 Refinement. A written response to the review, "explaining: the agency's agreement or disagreement, the actions the agency has undertaken or will undertake in response to the report, and (if applicable) the reasons the agency believes those actions satisfy any key concerns". The case workflow's record of each finding's fate (`notes/case_pipeline.md` 9.7) is this response; the peer review packages of `notes/what_a_case_delivers.md` item 5 are the charge.

7.3 **Reader testing.**

7.3.1 Plain-language testing (opened, 4.2): "Plan to test at least twice." Paraphrase testing, one reader at a time: "Conduct between 6 to 9 interviews"; the reader reads to a cue and says in their own words what the section means, and is asked "What would you do if you got this document?"; "Avoid yes or no questions." Usability testing suits longer documents where finding information matters; controlled comparative studies come last, at scale. Its examples show why the test cannot be replaced by asking whether the text is clear: readers who "all said that it was clear" would still have acted wrongly because they read a term of art differently.

7.3.2 European Commission (12 January 2009), *Guideline on the readability of the labelling and package leaflet of medicinal products for human use*, revision 1. Opened. The law requires that a medicine's leaflet "shall reflect the results of consultations with target patient groups to ensure that it is legible, clear and easy to use" (Directive 2001/83/EC, article 59(3), as amended). User testing "aims to identify whether or not the information as presented, conveys the correct messages to those who read it", and is done on "a full mock-up of the leaflet in the colours and style and on the paper as used", that is, on the document as delivered. Its pass mark, that most testers can both find each key point and show they understand it, is recalled from the method's literature and is not in the text read.

7.3.3 A language model as reader. Bisbee J, Clinton JD, Dorff C, Kenkel B, Larson JM (2024), Synthetic replacements for human survey data? The perils of large language models, *Political Analysis* 32(4):401-416 (in `research/refs.toml`; title confirmed in Crossref): language models answering as personas are not reliable stand-ins for people. A model's cold read is a check of the text against the facts and the plain-language rules, worth doing, but it is not a reader test, and a document that had only that is labelled so (CORE rule 1).

7.4 **Inspection.** One inspector finds about half a spreadsheet's errors, a group about 80 per cent (Panko 2000, in `research/refs.toml`); Fagan's inspections of design and code, with a moderator, roles and a checklist, are the engineering form (Fagan 1976, in the shared library). The IPCC's rule of writing individual assessments before discussion (5.2.2) is the same independence applied to judgement.

7.5 What these sources ask, together. The assurer is not the maker (AQuA chapters 3 and 5); the review's charge is written before the review, asks that the uncertainties and their effect on the conclusions be clear, and excludes policy advice (OMB 2004); every comment gets a written fate (OMB 2004); the document is tested as delivered, the rendered PDF (European Commission 2009, which the layout review of `notes/pdf_layout_review.md` already does for form); a reader test with 6 to 9 people from the audience is the established check of comprehension, a human-cost step and, by the guidelines' own limit, worth it when many will read the document (plain-language guidelines; van der Bles et al. 2019; CHSRF); and each document states its assurance state, what review it has had and what it has not (AQuA 9.1.3, 9.3; the Office of Impact Analysis's statement of sufficiency).

## 8 What this asks of the documents stage

The design belongs to the design seat; these are the requirements the literature sets for it, each with its sources.

8.1 The three documents are layers of one report, each complete for its reader, each read alone by some readers (CHSRF 1:3:25; Government Analysis Function 2018; the Dashboard and Detailed IA; the Green Book's executive summary; A-4's executive summary). Every layer carries the common elements of 2.8.1 at its own depth, and the numbers in every layer are the same facts.

8.2 The one-page summary leads with what the result means for the decision, then the headline: the expected net benefits (or, in a case that estimates benefits only, the expected benefits, said to be such) with the 90 per cent interval, and the chance that the sign is as stated, in words with the number beside them (CHSRF; plain-language guidelines; A-4; Mastrandrea et al. 2010; Budescu et al. 2009). It translates the result to one person or household (RFF 3.3; the owner's rule 3), names the largest effects left out and the value they would need to change the answer (A-4 threshold analysis; Green Book switching values), says who bears the costs (the owner's rule 12; A-4 distributional effects), and states its review status (7.5). At most one figure, titled with its takeaway and labelled directly (Urban Institute).

8.3 The policy paper follows RFF Working Paper 21-10's sequence with the four departures of 3.5.2, the CHSRF's ordering of implications before approach, and the policy-study form of an argument from problem to options to recommendation (Young and Quinn 2002, recalled). No equation in the body; every figure with an active title, a source line and direct labels (Urban Institute; Schwabish 2014, 2020); caveats beside the points they qualify and the vital ones up front (Government Analysis Function 2018); a discussion section that lists what is omitted and every known bias, in both directions (RFF 3.3).

8.4 The technical appendix is written as an academic paper: a structured abstract (CHEERS item 2); every input in a table with its distribution and source (CHEERS 22, the case's `inputs.toml`); the model described in full, with its formulas and where it can be obtained (CHEERS 16; AEA); the accounting statement, with undiscounted annual streams, transfers apart and the three categories of effect (A-4; A-94); the methods and effects of the uncertainty analysis (CHEERS 20, 24); distributional effects (CHEERS 19); limitations, from `CASE.md`'s Limits (CHEERS 26); funding and conflicts (CHEERS 27, 28); a data and code availability statement mapping outputs to exhibits (AEA); and a reporting checklist that maps each CHEERS item and each A-4 element to its section, or marks it "not applicable" or "not done in this preset", with the reason (CHEERS's own instruction; the AQuA Book's remaining risks). For each judgement input, a traceable account of the evidence and agreement behind it (Mastrandrea et al. 2010).

8.5 The rules of uncertainty language are one list, applied by every document: the centre is the mean of the draws; the interval is the 5th to 95th percentile; a calibrated word is never printed without its number; the quality of evidence is stated apart from the width; the precision follows the most uncertain input; reciprocal framing where a chance could be read as loaded; and figures are drawn from the draws (5.9).

8.6 Numbers and the words that depend on them come from the facts only, through one build script from a clean checkout, and each document records the run and commit that made it (6.7). The checks are the typed-number refusal the pipeline already names, a check of number-dependent words against the facts, and the round trip from the rendered PDF to the facts.

8.7 Review: the referee agent is the assurer, distinct from the writer (AQuA chapters 3 and 5); its charge is written before the review (OMB 2004); the review checks the documents against the facts, the limits and the reading rules above, and each finding has its written fate (OMB 2004; `notes/case_pipeline.md` 9.7). A practitioner's reading (the owner's rule 13) and a reader test with the audience are human steps: an overnight run delivers a draft ready for them, not a draft that has had them, and each document's status line says which reviews it has had (AQuA 9.1.3; CORE rule 1).

8.8 Proportionality across presets: the set of documents and their depth vary by preset; the elements each document carries do not, and an element a preset skips is named as skipped, with the reason (A-4's tiers; Green Book 1.8; AQuA 3.2; CHEERS "Not applicable"; the plain-language guidelines' limit on testing). A Quick estimate's one page and appendix carry the same headline, interval, omissions and status line as a Full analysis's, from fewer and wider inputs.

8.9 Departures from the owner's words (CORE rule 6).

8.9.1 "The RFF paper" is a working paper written for semi-technical readers; RFF's own non-specialist version is its 1,400-word issue brief. The 20-page paper takes the working paper's structure in the plainer register the 1:3:25 rule asks for (3.5.3).

8.9.2 The RFF paper's headline is a benefit-cost ratio at central values. The project's paper leads with expected net benefits and their interval (3.5.2).

8.9.3 The established form of his "1-pager executive summary" is the 1:3:25 rule's one page of main messages, which tells the reader what the findings mean for their decision rather than summarizing them; the longer executive summary of that rule is the first section of the policy paper, as RFF's two-page summary is. This sharpens what goes on his one page without changing its length; whether to adopt it is his to say if the design seat would change the page's purpose.

8.10 What the literature lets the stage leave out (CORE rule 11, deletion first). No fourth document: the 1:3:25 rule's three-page executive summary is the policy paper's opening section, and the reporting checklist lives in the appendix, not in a separate compliance file (4.1; 8.4). No per-document facts: one `facts.json` serves all three, so one set of checks covers them (6.7). No approximation of the mean from percentiles, since the draws exist (3.5.2). No reader test where few will read the document (4.2.2). No second uncertainty vocabulary per document: the list of 8.5 is shared.
