# Why broad but shallow: what the literature says

Written 2026-11-10 for the owner's reprogramming of the project's agents to build cost-benefit analyses broad but shallow: every effect of a policy gets a simple estimate a person can follow, and only a few get a small model. Every quotation below was machine-checked against the `text.txt` of the stored work (`cost_benefit_engine.quotes.quote_in`), except where a line says "recalled". A page is the printed page where the stored copy shows one; where it shows none (a web page, a working paper) the line says so. Stored works are in `research/sources/<key>/`, and each has an entry in `research/refs.toml`.

## 1. Which say that a conclusion resting on several simple, independent lines of argument is more reliable than one resting on a single elaborate model? (Q1)

1.1. Levins 1966, p. 423 (stored). The earliest and most quoted statement. He treats one problem with several models that differ in their simplifications and share one biological assumption: "Then, if these models, despite their different assumptions, lead to similar results we have what we can call a robust theorem which is relatively free of the details of the model. Hence our truth is the intersection of independent lies." The quotation the brief gave is verbatim ("our truth is the intersection of independent lies").

1.2. Wimsatt 1981, Springer volume pp. 66 and 67 (stored). He gives the argument its arithmetic. A chain of reasoning is weaker than its weakest link, since any link may fail; alternatives in parallel behave the other way: "With independent alternative ways of deriving a result, the result is always surer than its weakest derivation." The failure probability of m independent alternatives, each failing with probability p0, is p0 to the power m, and he concludes: "Adding alternatives (or redundancy, as it is often called) always increases reliability". The chapter opens (p. 61) with Peirce's version: philosophy should "trust rather to the multitude and variety of its arguments than to the conclusiveness of any one". This is the direct support for the owner's six-streams argument.

1.3. The condition on 1.1 and 1.2, which is mine and not a quotation. Both rest on the alternatives being independent. Benefit streams that share an assumption (the same discount rate, the same affected population, the same effect size from one study) fail together, and the arithmetic of 1.2 does not apply to them. An agent should say which assumption each stream shares with the others.

1.4. Karnofsky 2014 (stored, a blog, no pages). He names the two styles. "Sequence thinking involves making a decision based on a single model of the world", and "Cluster thinking – generally the more common kind of thinking – involves approaching a decision from multiple perspectives". Cluster thinking "limits the weight each can carry based on robustness", so that "any chain of reasoning involving high uncertainty is essentially disallowed from making too much difference to the final decision, regardless of the magnitude of effect it points to". He also grants the other side: "Sequence thinking has the advantage of making one's assumptions and beliefs highly transparent" (the text has a curly apostrophe), and says each is useful (the post's section "Sequence thinking can generate robust conclusions that then inform cluster thinking"). He is an advocate of cluster thinking for decisions, not an experimenter; the post argues and cites no measurement.

1.5. Armstrong 2001, Combining forecasts (stored, PDF pp. 1 and 2). Measured support for combining several methods: "Compared with errors of the typical individual forecast, combining reduces errors. In 30 empirical comparisons, the reduction in ex ante errors for equally weighted combined forecasts averaged about 12.5% and ranged from 3 to 24 percent." It says why: "Combining can reduce errors arising from faulty assumptions, bias, or mistakes in data." This is combining forecasts of one quantity, not several benefit streams; it supports the project's rule 9 more than the six-streams argument.

1.6. Pindyck 2013 (stored, NBER working paper, p. 15 and abstract). He proposes a shallow alternative to the integrated assessment models and says when it carries a conclusion: "if they are sufficiently large and robust to reasonable ranges for those parameters, it would support a stringent abatement policy". He then accepts the price: "Of course this approach does not carry the perceived precision that comes from an IAM-based analysis, but that perceived precision is illusory." The abstract: "IAM-based analyses of climate policy create a perception of knowledge and precision, but that perception is illusory and misleading." Pindyck 2015 (stored, p. 2 of the PDF) repeats it: "perception of knowledge and precision that is illusory, and can fool policy-makers into thinking". This is item 14 of the brief.

1.7. Lempert, Popper and Bankes 2003 (recalled, not stored; no quote). Robust decision making chooses the option that performs acceptably across many futures instead of the best option under one forecast. I recall this from the report's description in catalogues; it is the same idea as 1.2 applied to decisions, and has not been read.

1.8. Tetlock 2005 (recalled, not stored; no quote). Foxes, who draw on many small ideas, forecast better than hedgehogs, who rest on one big idea. Karnofsky 2014 names it (its section "Why Cluster Thinking?" refers to "the related concept of foxes vs. hedgehogs"), which is the only checked trace here.

## 2. Which say that added complexity raises error, or adds no accuracy, where the world is uncertain, and by how much? (Q2)

2.1. Green and Armstrong 2015 (stored as the working paper of January 2015, PDF pp. 2, 13 and 14; the published article may differ in wording). The headline figure is the 27 per cent: "None of the papers provide a balance of evidence that complexity improves forecast accuracy. Complexity increases forecast error by 27 percent on average in the 25 papers with quantitative comparisons." The count: 97 comparisons in 32 papers, of which "Eighty-four percent of the comparisons found that forecasts from simple methods were more accurate than, or similarly accurate to, those from complex methods." The conclusion: "Remarkably, no matter what type of forecasting method is used, complexity harms accuracy."

2.2. What 2.1 does and does not show, since an agent will be challenged on it. It is about forecasting accuracy, not about cost-benefit models. "Simple" there means understandable to the decision-maker, and the authors say their simple methods are "not in all cases sophisticatedly simple", so it does not license crude. The 27 per cent is an average over 25 papers, not a rate to apply to a case.

2.3. Gigerenzer and Brighton 2009 (stored, pp. 110, 111 and 119). The statistical reason. A less-is-more effect is defined as: "Less-is-more effects: More information or computation can decrease accuracy; therefore, minds rely on simple heuristics in order to be more accurate than strategies that use more information and time." Across 20 data sets tested by cross-validation, "Averaged across all data sets, tallying achieved a higher predictive accuracy than multiple regression", because "Regression tended to overfit the data". The mechanism is the bias-variance trade-off: "The more flexible the model, the more likely it is to capture not only the underlying pattern but unsystematic patterns such as noise", and "This is an example of how a biased model can lead to more accurate predictions than an unbiased model." No percentage is given for a general case: whether simple beats complex depends on the environment, and the paper asks "in which environments" it does (its section 1.1).

2.4. Dawes 1979 (stored, pp. 571 and 574). Equal weights against fitted weights: "In fact, unit (i.e., equal) weighting is quite robust for making such predictions." The reason: "b weights are notoriously unstable", and fitted weights beat unit weights on a new sample only when the ratio of observations to predictors is high (the stored text, an extract of a two-column page, reads "as high as IS or 20 to 1" on p. 574, a misread of 15; the line is split across columns, so it is not quoted whole). A cost-benefit input drawn from a handful of studies is far below that ratio.

2.5. Box 1976 (recalled, not stored, so not checked). The brief's two quotes, "Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a 'correct' one by excessive elaboration" and "overelaboration and overparameterization is often the mark of mediocrity", are widely attributed to the "Worrying selectively" section of this paper (a web search returned secondary summaries pointing to it). They are given here as unverified. Do not put them in an agent's prompt as quotations until the text is read, for instance by a seat with library access.

2.6. Saltelli and Funtowicz 2014 (stored, web page): "Complex and often overparameterized mechanistic models should be used only for more speculative investigations outside of the policy realm."

2.7. Read 1898, p. 272 (stored, OCR of the first edition): "Even in reasoning upon some subjects, it is a mistake to aim at an unattainable precision. It is better to be vaguely right than exactly wrong." The brief's quotation is exact. The OCR may misread a word elsewhere; the checked sentences read clearly.

## 3. Which say that, in a contested, wicked or adversarial policy setting, transparency and the reader's ability to check the work matter more than precision? (Q3)

3.1. Saltelli and Funtowicz 2014 (stored; the web page has no page numbers). This is the nearest to the owner's argument, and it is on a policy setting. Their thesis: "stringent criteria of transparency must be adopted when models are used as a basis for policy assessments". Rule 5: "Rule 5: Aim for transparency. Stakeholders should be able to make sense of and, if possible, replicate the results of the analysis." Its corollary: "simple or parsimonious model representations are better than more “sophisticated” or complex models, when they are being used for policy impact assessments". On the reader's ability to check: "If the models are too complex to be assessed by well-informed nonexperts, then there is no way to know what biases they may contain or how plausible their results might be." On who benefits (rule 1): "Rule 1 prescribes that questions must be raised about who benefits from the model and what motivations and incentives animate model developers." Their example of the failure: a nuclear waste model that "includes 286 interacting submodules, thus making it inherently incomprehensible even to experts", and the maxim "Models should illuminate complexity, not create it." On the cost of a hidden assumption to the adversarial case: "once an unacknowledged assumption is publicly exposed, the entire model is effectively falsified for public purposes, even if it has valuable insights to impart". Their closing view: "The commitment to transparency and parsimony that the rules demand will encourage modelers themselves to focus on parameters, inputs, assumptions, and relationships that are well constrained and understood."

3.2. Rittel and Webber 1973 (stored, pp. 162 and 163). Why a policy problem is not one where precision settles the matter. "Wicked problems have no stopping rule" (p. 162, the heading of their second property), and for such problems "For wicked planning problems, there are no true or false answers." Who judges: "many parties are equally equipped, interested, and/or entitled to judge the solutions, although none has the power to set formal decision rules to determine correctness". So the reader's ability to follow and challenge an argument is part of how a wicked problem is decided. They do not write about transparency as such; the step from their text to the claim is mine.

3.3. Green and Armstrong 2015 (stored, PDF pp. 2 and 14), on incentives: "forecasters’ clients may be reassured by incomprehensibility" is one of three reasons they offer for the popularity of complexity, and their advice is that "forecasting procedures should always be simple enough for forecast users to understand".

3.4. Pindyck 2013 and 2015 (stored), 1.6 above: a precise-looking model can mislead the policy-maker ("can fool policy-makers into thinking").

3.5. Karnofsky 2011 (stored, a blog, no pages). The nearest to a dissent in the set; the owner's agents should know it. On explicit single-formula estimates he says: "But when the formulas are too rough, I think the loss of accuracy outweighs the gains to transparency." His point is that a transparent formula that omits most of what is known can be worse than judgement; he adds that expected-value ranking "doesn’t seem to allow rewarding charities for transparency or penalizing them for opacity" (the text has a curly apostrophe). It is a caution against a crude estimate with no prior, not against simple estimates checked against several perspectives, but a referee may cite it.

3.6. Karnofsky 2014 (stored): "Sequence thinking has the advantage of making one’s assumptions and beliefs highly transparent" (1.4), the same trade-off from the other side.

## 4. What decision rule do items 15 and Saltelli 2008 give for which uncertain quantities deserve a fuller model, and when to stop? (Q4)

4.1. What could not be checked. Felli and Hazen 1998, Howard 1988, Claxton 1999 and Saltelli and others 2008 are not stored, so none is quoted. What follows from them is recalled and labelled so. A seat with library access should read them before the rule is put in a prompt as theirs.

4.2. Saltelli and others 2008 (recalled). Global sensitivity analysis ranks the uncertain inputs by how much of the variance of the result each explains (the first-order index, and the total-effect index for interactions). The setting called factors prioritisation asks which input, if learned exactly, would reduce the output variance most; the setting called factors fixing asks which inputs can be fixed at any value in their range without changing the output, so they need no model. This is a rule about the variance of the output; it does not by itself say when to stop.

4.3. Felli and Hazen 1998 (recalled). They compare measures of how much each input matters, and conclude that the expected value of perfect information for a parameter combines both the probability that learning it would change the decision and the size of the gain if it did (the recall of a catalogue abstract). A parameter that cannot change the decision has zero value however wide its interval.

4.4. Claxton 1999 (recalled). Whether a result is statistically significant is irrelevant to the decision; what matters is the expected net benefit of the options and the expected value of learning more. More research or modelling on an input is worthwhile only while its expected value exceeds its cost.

4.5. Howard 1988 (recalled). Decision analysis proceeds in phases: first a deterministic phase in which each input is varied across its range to see which ones can change the decision; then a probabilistic phase for the few that can; then an informational phase asking what it is worth to learn more. This is the rule the brief describes as "deterministic sensitivity first, probabilistic treatment only for what matters".

4.6. What stored texts add. Strong and others 2014 (`strong_2014_estimating`, section "Further Research"): "The partial expected value of perfect information (EVPI) quantifies the expected benefit of learning the values of uncertain parameters in a decision model", and the stopping bound: "it represents only an upper bound on the expected value of undertaking research to reduce decision uncertainty", the finer measure being the expected value of sample information for a particular data collection. So an input whose partial value is below the cost of studying it should not get a fuller model. The European Commission's 2014 guide (PDF p. 70): "The use of switching values in sensitivity analysis allows making some judgements on the risk of the project and the opportunity of undertaking risk‑preventing actions." The CADTH 2017 guidelines (PDF p. 59) say that value-of-information analyses "support decision-makers’ assessments of the return on investment of further research when specific parameters or groups of parameters are identified as being responsible for a substantial portion of the total decision uncertainty". Saltelli and Funtowicz 2014, rule 7 (stored): "Rule 7: Focus the analysis. Do not do perfunctory sensitivity analyses, merely changing one factor at a time." and rule 4: "Rule 4: Find sensitive assumptions before they find you."

4.7. The rule an agent can follow, from 4.2 to 4.6 (the synthesis is mine, the parts are cited above). First give every effect a simple estimate and a wide interval. Then, for each, find the value its input would have to take for the net benefit to change sign (the switching value, 4.6), and rank the inputs by how much of the variance of net benefit each explains (4.2). Build a fuller model only for an effect whose uncertainty can change the decision, and stop when the value of the further work (at most the partial value of perfect information, 4.6) falls below its cost (4.4). If the policy's sign holds with any of the effects zeroed (1.2), the further work has no value for the decision.

## 5. For an agent's prompt

5.1. A conclusion that rests on several independent routes is surer than any one of them: with m independent alternatives each failing with probability p0, the chance all fail is p0 to the power m (Wimsatt 1981, pp. 66-67; Levins 1966, p. 423: "our truth is the intersection of independent lies"); the routes must not share an assumption.
5.2. Across 97 comparisons in 32 papers, "Complexity increases forecast error by 27 percent on average" in the 25 with numbers, and simple methods were as accurate or better in 84 per cent (Green and Armstrong 2015, working paper, pp. 2 and 13; forecasting evidence, and "simple" means understandable, not crude).
5.3. A fitted model with many parameters overfits: "The more flexible the model, the more likely it is to capture not only the underlying pattern but unsystematic patterns such as noise" (Gigerenzer and Brighton 2009, p. 119), and equal weights are "quite robust" against fitted ones (Dawes 1979, p. 571).
5.4. In policy work, "stringent criteria of transparency must be adopted", and "simple or parsimonious model representations are better than more “sophisticated” or complex models" (Saltelli and Funtowicz 2014, Issues in Science and Technology 30(2)); a model a reader cannot assess hides its biases.
5.5. A hidden assumption is a liability: "once an unacknowledged assumption is publicly exposed, the entire model is effectively falsified for public purposes" (Saltelli and Funtowicz 2014, rule 4).
5.6. Precision can mislead: a model's "perception of knowledge and precision" can be "illusory and misleading", and a simple argument whose result is "robust to reasonable ranges" of its parameters can carry the policy (Pindyck 2013, abstract and p. 15).
5.7. Where there are no true or false answers and "many parties are equally equipped, interested, and/or entitled to judge", the argument a reader can follow and challenge is the one that persuades (Rittel and Webber 1973, p. 163).
5.8. Give a fuller model only to an effect whose uncertainty can change the decision, and stop when the expected value of learning more falls below its cost: partial expected value of perfect information "represents only an upper bound on the expected value of undertaking research" (Strong and others 2014); "It is better to be vaguely right than exactly wrong" (Read 1898, p. 272).
