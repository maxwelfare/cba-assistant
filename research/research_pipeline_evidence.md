# Evidence for the literature-research pipeline (run 2026-10-29)

Method note: every source below was found by search or fetch in this run. WebFetch caps quotes at 125 characters, so quotes are short fragments of the abstract or page, not whole sentences. Items marked UNVERIFIED are from search snippets only.

## 1. How AI tools fail at literature review

Answer: Fabricated citations are common in parametric (no-retrieval) models and fall with newer models and retrieval. Retrieval-based tools miss papers (recall), and models favour already highly cited papers. I found no peer-reviewed head-to-head study of commercial "AI literature review" products on source selection, and I did not find the specific "bad paper selection" article he read.

- Walters & Wilder (2023), "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT", Scientific Reports, doi:10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5. Quote: "Within this set of documents, 55% of the GPT-3.5 citations but just 18% of the GPT-4 citations are fabricated." (via PMC10484980). Search summary: 42 topics, 636 references; 43% of real GPT-3.5 and 24% of real GPT-4 citations had substantive errors (UNVERIFIED, snippet). 2023 models, so dated.
- Algaba et al. (2024), "Large Language Models Reflect Human Citation Patterns with a Heightened Citation Bias", arXiv:2405.15739 (no journal reference listed), doi:10.48550/arXiv.2405.15739. Fetched abstract says there is "a remarkable similarity between human and LLM citation patterns" but a stronger bias toward highly cited papers, which persists after controls, for GPT-4, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5. Measured on parametric recall only, not on retrieval tools. A follow-up, arXiv:2504.02767 (about 275,000 GPT-4o references), reports the same Matthew-effect result (UNVERIFIED, search snippet only).
- Agarwal et al. (2025), "LitLLMs, LLMs for Literature Review: Are we there yet?", TMLR, arXiv:2412.15249. Search-result text (UNVERIFIED, not fetched): finding all papers a human review cited "will require new querying strategies"; keyword plus embedding search beats either alone; planning-based prompting reduces but does not eliminate hallucinated references.
- Sahu, Charlin & Pal (2026), "Rethinking Literature Search Evaluation: Deep Research Helps, and Human Citation Lists Are Not a Ground Truth", arXiv:2605.29234 (preprint, 28 May 2026). Fetched summary: a deep-research pipeline that expands through bibliographies raises recall from under 20% (API-only search) to over 80%; only about half of human citations were judged moderately relevant or better by an LLM judge. Caveat: it argues recall against a human reference list is itself a flawed target.
- Not verified: vendor and blog comparisons (e.g. Elicit's own evaluation reporting 96.9% screening sensitivity, elicit.com/blog/evaluating-elicit-slr) are vendor-reported. The HKUST library guide (library.hkust.edu.hk/sc/trust-ai-lit-rev/) says Scite, Elicit and Consensus draw mainly on Semantic Scholar abstracts, which bounds what they can select. That is a plausible mechanism for poor selection but not a measured failure rate.

Strength: moderate for fabricated citations and citation bias; weak for "deep research tools select poorly" (no independent benchmark found).

## 2. Retrieval-grounded agents that do better

Answer: Retrieval grounding sharply cuts fabricated citations and, on the authors' own benchmarks, matches or beats experts. These are developer-run evaluations on benchmarks the developers built.

- Asai et al., "OpenScholar: Synthesizing Scientific Literature with Retrieval-augmented LMs", arXiv:2411.14199 (Nov 2024; a later Nature version exists per search results, UNVERIFIED), doi:10.48550/arXiv.2411.14199. Quote: "While GPT4o hallucinates citations 78 to 90% of the time, OpenScholar achieves citation accuracy on par with human experts." Also "OpenScholar-GPT4o improves GPT-4o's correctness by 12%." Datastore: 45 million open-access papers; benchmark ScholarQABench. The authors acknowledge retrieval does not always find the most relevant papers (secondary summary, UNVERIFIED).
- Skarlinski et al. (FutureHouse), "Language agents achieve superhuman synthesis of scientific knowledge", arXiv:2409.13740 (Sep 2024), doi:10.48550/arXiv.2409.13740. Quote: PaperQA2 "matches or exceeds subject matter expert performance" on three literature tasks; it surpasses human performance on the authors' LitQA2 benchmark. Contradiction detection: 2.34 +/- 1.99 per paper, 70% validated by experts. I did not see a recall figure for paper selection in what I fetched.

Strength: moderate (two strong developer evaluations, no independent replication found in this run).

## 3. Citation chasing versus database searching

Answer: In one complex-evidence review, most sources came from chasing references, not from the protocol's database and hand searches. Snowballing from a good start set is a credible primary strategy. Automated chasing tools exist; I found no recall measurements for them.

- Greenhalgh & Peacock (2005), "Effectiveness and efficiency of search methods in systematic reviews of complex evidence: audit of primary sources", BMJ 331:1064-1065, doi:10.1136/bmj.38636.593461.68 (PMC1283190). Quotes: "Only 30% of sources were obtained from the protocol defined at the outset of the study"; "Fifty one per cent were identified by 'snowballing'"; "24% by personal knowledge or personal contacts." (495 sources, one review; the categories overlap in the paper's text as shown.)
- Wohlin (2014), "Guidelines for snowballing in systematic literature studies and a replication in software engineering", EASE '14, doi:10.1145/2601248.2601268. Per search results, it proposes snowballing as a first search strategy and an alternative to database search, tested by replicating a published review (UNVERIFIED beyond title; the abstract text was not retrievable beyond two short fragments).
- Haddaway, Grainger & Gray (2022), "Citationchaser: A tool for transparent and efficient forward and backward citation chasing in systematic searching", Research Synthesis Methods, doi:10.1002/jrsm.1563. R package and Shiny app using the Lens.org API. No recall figures found.
- Hirt et al. (2023), "Citation tracking for systematic literature searching: A scoping review", Research Synthesis Methods 14(3):563-579, doi:10.1002/jrsm.1635: 47 studies (1985-2021). Recall figures not retrieved. (UNVERIFIED, from search summary.)
- Sahu et al. (above, arXiv:2605.29234) is the newest evidence that bibliography expansion raises recall (under 20% to over 80%).

Strength: moderate. One audit, one review; mechanism is plausible, and generalisation to economics topics is untested.

## 4. Rapid and scoping reviews

Answer (one line each; Garritty and Arksey quotes come from the guidance text itself, read from the PDFs):

- Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group. Garritty et al. (2021), "Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group offers evidence-informed guidance to conduct rapid reviews", J Clin Epidemiol 130:13-22, doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.10.007 (the abstract itself does not state the specifics). The specifics are from the interim guidance PDF dated 23 March 2020 (methods.cochrane.org, cochrane_rr_-_guidance-23mar2020-final.pdf), which I could not match to the 2021 paper's final wording; any later update was not checked.
  - Search: "Always search Cochrane CENTRAL, MEDLINE ... and Embase", 1-2 extra specialised databases at most, "Limit literature searches to English language", limit grey literature, and screen reference lists only after screening of the abstracts and full texts.
  - Screening: "Use two reviewers for dual screen of at least 20% (ideally more) of abstracts", one reviewer for the rest, and "Use a second reviewer to screen all excluded abstracts"; same pattern for full text.
  - Appraisal: "Use a single reviewer to rate risk of bias, with full verification of all judgements" by a second reviewer, limited to the most important outcomes.
- Scoping reviews, Arksey & O'Malley (2005), "Scoping studies: towards a methodological framework", Int J Soc Res Methodol 8(1):19-32, doi:10.1080/1364557032000119616 (text read from White Rose PDF). Search: "the whole point of scoping the field is to be as comprehensive as possible", using several sources including reference lists. Appraisal: a scoping study "does not seek to assess quality of evidence". The paper does not specify single or dual screening.
- JBI: Peters et al. (2020), "Updated methodological guidance for the conduct of scoping reviews", JBI Evid Synth 18(10):2119-2126, doi:10.11124/JBIES-20-00167 (PMID 33038124). The abstract says the guidance covers "the role of methodological appraisal" only in secondary descriptions; I could not read the JBI manual itself, so JBI's position on appraisal and on single versus dual screening is UNVERIFIED. Secondary guides say appraisal is optional and that two independent screeners are recommended.
- PRISMA-ScR: Tricco et al. (2018), Ann Intern Med 169(7):467-473, doi:10.7326/M18-0850. It is a reporting checklist (20 essential items, 2 optional), not a conduct standard; I did not read the full text.

Strength: strong for the Cochrane and Arksey wording; the JBI details are not verified.

## 5. LLMs as title/abstract screeners

Answer: A single LLM reaches high sensitivity but low specificity, which suits use as a second reviewer or a pre-filter, not a replacement for a human who checks the exclusions. Results vary by review and prompt.

- Tran et al. (2024), "Sensitivity and Specificity of Using GPT-3.5 Turbo Models for Title and Abstract Screening in Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses", Ann Intern Med 177(6):791-799, doi:10.7326/M23-3389 (PMID 38768452). 5 reviews, 22,665 citations. Quotes: balanced rule "sensitivities ranged from 81.1% to 96.5% and specificities ranged from 25.8% to 80.4%"; sensitive rule "sensitivities ranged from 94.6% to 99.8% and specificities ranged from 2.2% to 46.6%". The model found 7 of 708 citations (1%) humans had missed (search snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- Dai et al. (2025), JMIR 27:e67488 (thoracic surgery; ChatGPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet against dual human screening). Search snippet only (UNVERIFIED): pooled sensitivity 0.73 and specificity 0.99 at first, rising to sensitivity 0.98 after prompt revision. The PDF fetch returned empty.
- Kim et al., J Med Artif Intell (meta-analysis of 14 LLM screening models; "sensitivity and specificity approaching 90%"). Search snippet only; year and details UNVERIFIED.
- Elicit's vendor claim (96.9% sensitivity) is not independent.

Strength: moderate for the pattern (high sensitivity, specificity depends on rule); older models (GPT-3.5) dominate the verified evidence.

## 6. Structured expert elicitation

Answer:
- IDEA: Hemming, Burgman, Hanea, McBride & Wintle (2018), "A practical guide to structured expert elicitation using the IDEA protocol", Methods Ecol Evol 9:169-180, doi:10.1111/2041-210X.12857. A four-step modified Delphi ('Investigate', 'Discuss', 'Estimate', 'Aggregate'), usable remotely on a modest budget. Evidence: Hemming et al. (2018), PLOS ONE, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0198468: 76 participants, 14 future Great Barrier Reef events, judgments scored after the events; the search summary says pooled group judgments almost always outperformed individuals (UNVERIFIED; the abstract's own concluding line, per the search summary, is "practical, cost-effective, and repeatable"). Hanea et al. (2017), Int J Forecasting 33:267-279, found IDEA did well against an equally weighted linear pool and a prediction market (snippet, UNVERIFIED).
- SHELF: Oakley & O'Hagan's Sheffield Elicitation Framework (v2.0), a facilitated group workshop that reaches one agreed distribution by behavioural aggregation (Gosling, in Dias et al. (eds), Elicitation, Springer 2018, ch. 4, doi:10.1007/978-3-319-65052-4_4; description from search results, not read). Accuracy evidence: only a clinical-trial comparison in which SHELF aggregation beat the individual experts and the classical method (arXiv:2001.11365, Williams, Wilson & Wilson; snippet, UNVERIFIED). I found no large accuracy dataset for SHELF.
- Cooke's classical model: experts answer calibration questions with known answers and are weighted by statistical accuracy and informativeness. Colson & Cooke (2017), "Cross validation for the classical model of structured expert judgment", Reliab Eng Syst Saf 163:109-120 (33 studies). In-sample, performance weighting beats equal weighting in all but 1 of 33 studies; out-of-sample there is a penalty, and performance weighting wins on their new index in 26 of 33 post-2006 studies (search summaries of the abstract; the TU Delft page failed to load, so UNVERIFIED verbatim).

Strength: moderate for Cooke (largest dataset, honest about out-of-sample penalty); weak to moderate for IDEA and SHELF.

## 7. Free bibliographic APIs (terms read from official pages unless marked)

- OpenAlex. Official help page (help.openalex.org/how-to-use-the-api/rate-limits-and-authentication, "Last updated October 15, 2026"): a key is not required for basic queries; a free key "raises your daily budget 10x" and is required for cursor paging past the first page. Per-second limits: 10 with no key, 30 with a free key, 50 on an annual plan; semantic search 1 per second; 429 on excess. An OpenAlex users-group post (groups.google.com/g/openalex-users/c/rI1GIAySpVQ) had announced that keys would be required from 13 Feb 2026, with 100,000 credits per day, 1 credit per single lookup and 10 per list query, and the email/polite pool retired. The October 2026 page is later and says basic keyless queries still work, so use the page, and verify before building on keyless access. Prices of paid plans: not retrieved.
- Semantic Scholar. Official page semanticscholar.org/product/api: most endpoints are public without a key, in a shared pool ("1000 requests per second shared among all unauthenticated users"), and an API key gives a dedicated "1 request per second on all endpoints" introductory limit. Endpoint paths for citations and references: could not confirm from the official docs (api-docs/graph returned only navigation); search results suggest /paper/{id}/citations and /paper/{id}/references (UNVERIFIED). A CASRAI guide reports uneven throttling and HTTP 500 errors (third party).
- Crossref REST API. Official page (crossref.org/documentation/retrieve-metadata/rest-api/access-and-authentication/): Public pool 5 requests per second, concurrency 1; Polite pool 10 per second, concurrency 3, joined by adding an email in `mailto` or the `agent` header, plus a descriptive User-Agent; Plus pool 150 per second (paid key). 429 on excess.
- PubMed E-utilities. NCBI Insights post (ncbiinsights.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2017/11/02/new-api-keys-for-the-e-utilities/): "any computer (IP address) that submits more than three E-utility requests per second will receive an error message"; with a key "your key will increase the limit to 10 requests/second". The main NCBI Bookshelf documentation page (NBK25497) returned a browser-verification page, so I stopped and did not work around it; that 2017 post could be out of date for 2026.
- Europe PMC. The official REST page (europepmc.org/RestfulWebService) returned HTTP 403 to my fetch; blocked, not worked around. Only third-party client docs: citations and references endpoints exist, fullTextXML returns open-access full text from PMC, no registration or key (rOpenSci client doc); rate limit terms UNVERIFIED.
- Unpaywall. The official page (unpaywall.org/api/v2) was reachable only as a one-word stub in my fetch. Search result text for it: requests need an email parameter, use limited to 100,000 calls per day, snapshot download for heavier use; `best_oa_location` and `oa_locations` fields. UNVERIFIED from the official page itself.
- Retraction Watch in Crossref. Official page (crossref.org/documentation/retrieve-metadata/retraction-watch/): retractions appear in the `update-to` field with `source` of `publisher` or `retraction-watch`; example `https://api.crossref.org/v1/works?filter=update-type:retraction`; a CSV in a GitLab repository is updated once per working day; Crossref acquired the database in September 2023. License not stated on the page I read.
- Coverage, OpenAlex versus Scopus and Web of Science. Culbert et al. (2025, Scientometrics; arXiv:2401.16359): on 16.8 million publications in all three, "OpenAlex has average source reference numbers and internal coverage rates comparable to both Web of Science and Scopus"; OpenAlex has more ORCIDs but fewer abstracts (fetched). Alperin et al. (2024), cited in search results as finding more publications in OpenAlex than Scopus, especially in underrepresented regions and languages, with metadata gaps (UNVERIFIED; the paper itself was not retrieved). Missing abstracts matter for an agent that screens on abstracts.

Strength: strong for OpenAlex, Crossref, E-utilities, Retraction Watch (official pages, quoted); weak for Semantic Scholar endpoints, Europe PMC and Unpaywall (official pages not readable). Terms change often; re-check at build time.
