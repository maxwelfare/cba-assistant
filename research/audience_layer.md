# Writing and laying out a policy document for a named audience: the established techniques

Research note, 2026-11-12; the design that uses it is `notes/audience_layer.md`. Every source is indexed in `research/refs.toml`; this copy carries its stored text only where its licence allows, and the source-fetcher fetches the rest from the address the index keeps. A quote in double marks was machine-checked against the stored `text.txt` and is followed by [key, place]. "Abstract only" means nothing beyond the stored abstract is claimed; "recalled" means not read this session and not quoted. Each section says what its evidence is: strong (replicated experiments or a binding standard), weak (few studies, small samples, or untested on officials) or folklore (practice with no test found).

## 1 Plain language

1.1 The law. The Plain Writing Act defines its object by the reader: "writing that is clear, concise, well-organized, and follows other best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience" [plain_writing_act_2010, sec. 3(3)]. Writing for a named audience is what the Act asks for.

1.2 The guidelines. "The first rule of plain language is: write for your audience." [plain_language_guidelines, principles]. A writer puts the bottom line first and the main idea before its exceptions, and uses the active voice, verbs for actions and short sections under headings that say something (quotes in `research/documents_stage_literature.md` 4.2).

1.3 The Air Force's writing handbook gives the rule a reason and an exception. "Audience analysis is critical because different audiences have different views on what evidence is convincing." [af_2015_tongue_quill, p. 18]. "In nearly every communication situation, you need to state your bottom line early in the message." [af_2015_tongue_quill, p. 54]. The exception is a hostile audience, which gets the support first and the bottom line last (the same chapter; read, not quoted).

1.4 Evidence: strong as a standard that binds the reader's own institution; the guidelines rest on usability testing, not on experiments with officials.

## 2 Risk and uncertainty

2.1 The FDA's user's guide gives rules a writer can apply to every number [fischhoff_2011_communicating, ch. 7, pp. 60-61]:

2.1.1 "Provide absolute risks, not just relative risks."

2.1.2 "Keep denominators constant for comparisons."

2.1.3 "Provide both positive and negative frames. People, particularly those who are less numerate, are unduly influenced by whether a treatment is described in positive or negative terms (e.g., survival rates versus mortality rates). Whenever possible, describe the risks and benefits using both frames."

2.2 Its test of a communication is the reader's decision: "A communication is adequate if it contains any information that might affect a significant fraction of users’ choices." [fischhoff_2011_communicating, ch. 4, p. 20]. Its rule on evidence: "communications should be evaluated — because even the best science cannot guarantee results." [fischhoff_2011_communicating, ch. 1, p. 2]. Of adaptations made without a research base it warns that such "tailoring is more likely to weaken the communication than improve it." [fischhoff_2011_communicating, ch. 3, p. 14].

2.3 Stating a range costs little trust. Across five experiments, "total n = 5,780", "we observed only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication" [vanderbles_2020_effects, abstract]. A number with its range is safer than a verbal hedge.

2.4 Framing that hides is named as a fault: misunderstanding "is created by nontransparent framing of information that is sometimes an unintentional result of lack of understanding but can also be a result of intentional efforts to manipulate or persuade people" [gigerenzer_2007_helping, abstract; abstract only].

2.5 The reader's own institutions set the same bar, the strongest ground for holding it in every edition.

2.5.1 OMB, for anything a federal agency disseminates: "``Objectivity'' includes whether disseminated information is being presented in an accurate, clear, complete, and unbiased manner." [omb_2002_information_quality, V.3.a].

2.5.2 GAO, for an audit report: "Objective means that the presentation of the report is balanced in content and tone." [gao_2024_yellow_book, 9.17].

2.5.3 The present administration, for every agency: science is to be "communicative of error and uncertainty", and where uncertainty is present "the degree of uncertainty should be communicated." [eo_14303_gold_standard_science, sec. 3(a) and 4(g)].

2.6 Evidence: strong for 2.1 and 2.3 (many experiments, one of them a field experiment); the standards of 2.5 are binding texts.

## 3 Classical rhetoric

3.1 The three appeals. "Of the modes of persuasion furnished by the spoken word there are three kinds. The first kind depends on the personal character of the speaker; the second on putting the audience into a certain frame of mind; the third on the proof, or apparent proof, provided by the words of the speech itself." [aristotle_rhetoric_roberts, I.2]. For a policy paper: character is who wrote it, what review it has had and whose data it stands on; frame of mind is the reader's own concerns, named in the reader's words; proof is the analysis. Only the second varies by audience.

3.2 Arrangement. "A speech has two parts. You must state your case, and you must prove it." [aristotle_rhetoric_roberts_book3, III.13]. This is bottom line first. The Roman six parts (opening, facts, division, proof, refutation, close) are recalled, with no handbook stored; what they add is the refutation, the answer to those who will resist, which the owner's rule 12 already requires.

3.3 Sentence form. Prose is "either free-running, with its parts united by nothing except the connecting words" or "compact and antithetical" [aristotle_rhetoric_roberts_book3, III.9]. The loose sentence puts the main clause first and is plain language's default. The periodic sentence holds it to the end, so the last words carry the weight: a writer uses one where a finding should land, and nowhere else.

3.4 Evidence: folklore with a long record. No experiment on officials was looked for or found; where rhetoric and the plain-language evidence overlap they agree.

## 4 Framing by audience

4.1 Two kinds of frame, which the design treats differently [druckman_2001_implications].

4.1.1 "an equivalency framing effect, examines how the use of different, but logically equivalent, words or phrases (e.g., 5% unemployment or 95% employment, 97% fat-free or 3% fat) causes individuals to alter their preferences."

4.1.2 "an emphasis framing effect—shows that by emphasizing a subset of potentially relevant considerations, a speaker can lead individuals to focus on these considerations when constructing their opinions."

4.2 Equivalent frames move stated choices. With 600 lives at risk, the sure programme that leaves 200 alive is chosen by 72 per cent when it is worded as people "saved. [72 percent]" and by 22 per cent when worded "400 people will die." [tversky_1981_framing, problems 1 and 2, N = 152 and 155; a jumbled scan, and see 7.3]. A meta-analysis of 136 papers finds "that the overall framing effect between conditions is of small to moderate size" [kuhberger_1998_framing, abstract; abstract only], and a replication across 36 samples counts it among the effects that "replicated consistently" [klein_2014_manylabs, abstract].

4.2.1 The effect goes when the reader sees the other frame: "the counter-framing condition eliminates the framing effect" [druckman_2004_political, results]. That is why the standing advice is both frames (2.1.3), and the IPCC's is "Consider reciprocal statements" (`mastrandrea_2010_ipcc`; `research/documents_stage_literature.md` 5.2.2).

4.2.2 On behaviour, as against stated choice, a Cochrane review finds that "both attribute and goal framing may have little if any consistent effect on health consumers' behaviour." [akl_2011_framing, abstract; abstract only].

4.2.3 Evidence: strong that a frame moves a stated choice between options, the act of a decision-maker reading a summary; weak on later behaviour.

4.3 Moral reframing is emphasis framing by the reader's values. The ground: "liberals consistently showed greater endorsement and use of the Harm/care and Fairness/reciprocity foundations compared to the other 3 foundations, whereas conservatives endorsed and used the 5 foundations more equally." [graham_2009_liberals, abstract; abstract only]. The technique: "a position an individual would not normally support is framed in a way that is consistent with that individual's moral values" [feinberg_2019_moral, abstract; abstract only], supported "across six studies involving diverse political issues, including same-sex marriage, universal health care, military spending" [feinberg_2015_gulf, abstract; abstract only].

4.3.1 The limits. Value-matched frames entrench more reliably than they persuade: "Studies 1 and 2 support the entrenching hypothesis." and "Only Study 2 partially supports the persuasion hypothesis." [day_2014_shifting, abstract; abstract only]. The field test cannot isolate it: "there is no treatment condition that does not use personalized moral reframing, meaning we cannot definitively attribute any effects observed to it." [kalla_2022_personalizing, methods; the preprint].

4.3.2 Evidence: weak for this use. The studies are survey experiments on partisans' attitudes; none tests an official reading an analysis.

4.4 Cultural cognition adds who speaks. "Individuals reflexively reject information inconsistent with their predispositions when they perceive that it is being advocated by experts whose values they reject and opposed by ones whose values they share." [kahan_2011_cultural, 5.3; the authors' manuscript]. The remedy offered: "communicators must attend to the cultural meaning as well as the scientific content of information." [same]. A writer names first, among the sources already cited, those the reader trusts. Evidence: moderate (large samples, one research group).

4.5 The FrameWorks Institute turns emphasis framing into a list of decisions. "Framing is the choices we make in what we say and how we say it." [frameworks_framing_101]. Its twelve frame elements are context, explanatory chains, examples, metaphors, messengers, narrative, numbers, order, solutions, tone, values and visuals [frameworks_whats_in_a_frame; the list read, not quoted]. Three of its rules bear here: "Lead with values." and "Explain causes before mentioning effects." [frameworks_order_matters]; and numbers "work best when used to reinforce a message explicitly stated in words, rather than left to stand alone." [frameworks_whats_in_a_frame]. Evidence: weak for this use: a practitioner's own research on public audiences, its studies not read.

## 5 Visual framing and design by audience

5.1 Visual framing. Rodriguez and Dimitrova (2011) name four levels at which a picture frames: what it shows, its style, what it connotes and the ideology it carries. Recalled; no free copy was found, so nothing is quoted (7.1).

5.2 Colour. Party colour is weak as a preference: "On non-Election Days, Republicans and Democrats preferred Republican red equally, and Republicans actually preferred Democratic blue more than Democrats did." [schloss_2014_politics_color, abstract; abstract only]. The field's review asks for "considerable work on boundary conditions, moderators, and real-world generalizability" before recommendations [elliot_2014_color, abstract; abstract only]. The Urban Institute keeps the pair for party data: "Use blue and red sparingly, usually only when comparing political data." [urban_graphics_styleguide, colour]. Evidence: weak; red and blue by party is a convention, not a finding about readers.

5.3 Typeface. Two experiments (987 and 1,069 participants) find "Serifs are rated as more conservative than sans serifs and bold as more conservative than italics." and that "Republicans rate nearly all typefaces as more conservative, while Democrats perceive them to be more liberal." [haenschen_2020_font, abstract and results; the accepted manuscript]. The first study's gap is about a third of a standard deviation, on a neutral phrase, not a document. Evidence: weak.

5.4 No study was found of how report design varies with a publisher's leaning or changes how officials read: three OpenAlex searches by the fetching seat, listed in its return, found nothing. The sample below is the main evidence, and it is small.

5.6 What the sample supports, all of it weak: one looker, 22 documents, and what publishers do, never how readers respond.

5.6.1 Navy is everyone's colour, on the right, the left, the centre and both parties' White Houses. It signals an official document, not a side.

5.6.2 Red as an accent appears on the right and in the present administration's documents, and on no left-leaning cover.

5.6.3 Photographs of the people served appear on left-leaning covers only.

5.6.4 Serif display type does not divide the think tanks; it does mark the present administration's own documents and its nearest think tank (the MAHA report and strategy, AFPI).

5.6.5 Seals, the flag and stars appear on government documents of both parties, and on AFPI's. On a third party's analysis they would claim an authorship it does not have.

5.6.6 Vocabulary, and what a title counts (taxpayers and patients, or people covered), differ far more plainly than any visual cue. Every publisher of every leaning opens with the answer in a box or a list.

5.7 The architecture. The design system's unit is the token: "An object with a $value property is a token." [w3c_2025_design_tokens, 5.1], with a type and a description, and "The same value can have multiple names or aliases." [w3c_2025_design_tokens, 3.8]. Themes are a companion module, "a method to work with design tokens in multiple contexts (such as “light mode” and “dark mode” color themes)", later sets overriding earlier [w3c_2025_design_tokens_resolver, abstract]. Both are a community group's final reports of 28 October 2025, "not a W3C Standard". The floor a palette must meet is legibility: "a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1" for text [wcag_2023_contrast].

## 6 What a writer or designer takes from this

6.1 Hold fixed, on strong evidence or a binding standard: the bottom line first; plain, active sentences; every number with its range; one denominator; the frame of each number, with both frames for the headline; nothing material to the choice left out.

6.2 Vary by audience, on weak evidence, each choice marked as judgement: which of the reader's concerns each finding is tied to, in the reader's own words; which trusted sources are named first; which findings get a box; the order; the tone.

6.3 Vary the look lightly: an accent colour, the display typeface, the cover's icon. The sample gives conventions, not effects, and the cues that claim authorship (seals, flags, a publisher's own dress) are not available.
