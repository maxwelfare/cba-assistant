# Two or more independent methods: the options and their best practices

Eventually I will want to set up a structured agent process for brainstorming different approaches to estimating outcomes based on known data" (the owner's message of 2026-10-31; `notes/owner_cba_rules.md`, rule 9).

## 1 What makes two methods independent

1.1 Unrelated sources of bias. The established name is triangulation: "The idea behind triangulation is that when we compare different approaches with assumed unrelated sources of bias, particularly if the expected direction of bias for some of the approaches is different, we would not expect to obtain the same estimates of the causal effect (unless all were unbiased)" (`lawlor_2016_triangulation`). The authors stress "the importance of being explicit about the expected direction of bias within each approach, whenever this is possible, and seeking to identify approaches that would be expected to bias the true causal effect in different directions" (the same source). Two refinements of his words follow from this:
1.1.1 A method is independent of another when its main sources of error differ, not when it merely has another name.
1.1.2 Two methods that disagree tell us something. A disagreement is a finding to explain, by the direction of each method's bias, before the two are combined.

1.2 Shared data or code makes two methods one. Of climate models, "The distance between models from the same institutions is often a factor of three to ten smaller than that to other models" (`knutti_2013_genealogy`). Two estimates that share their data, their model or a key assumption count as one method. The project's rule 9 already says the same of several studies of one kind: they are combined by meta-analysis, not treated as independent methods.

1.3 The forecasting literature agrees, and adds a number: "To improve forecasting accuracy, combine forecasts derived from methods that differ substantially and draw from different sources of information. When feasible, use five or more methods" (`armstrong_2001_combining`, abstract). It also says "adding more methods leads to diminishing rates of improvement" (the same source). Armstrong counts methods that differ substantially, so variants of one method count as one (1.2). In a mixture the count matters twice: weights given per variant let the number of variants kept set the result. Of climate ensembles, "all models are treated equally, thus giving more weight to those who have submitted multiple versions or share their code", and the authors propose to "down weight models that have very similar control biases to avoid biased projections from near duplicate models" (`knutti_2013_genealogy`). The weight therefore goes to each independent method, and its variants share it (3.3).

## 2 How to generate the methods

2.1 Each generator works alone first, and the lists are pooled afterwards. A nominal group is a set of people who generate ideas alone and whose lists are pooled. Of the 22 brainstorming experiments in Diehl and Stroebe's review, 18 "reported the performance of nominal groups to be superior to that of real groups" (`diehl_1987_productivity`, Table 1). Their own experiments found that "production blocking accounted for most of the productivity loss of real brainstorming groups" (the same source). Production blocking is the loss that comes from members waiting their turn to speak. The same principle is the first step of the nominal group technique (Delbecq and Van de Ven 1971, recalled) and of the IDEA protocol, in which each expert estimates alone before any discussion (`hemming_2018_practical`; the library marks its evidence base unverified). For agents, the equivalent is helpers that are blind to one another, to the author's list and to the methods the case's design already names. The last matters most: a design that already names its methods can leave out a family its author knows and has set aside. The project's implementation options and its listers of effects already use this form (`notes/briefs/author_fast.md` 5.1; `.claude/agents/effects-lister.md`).

2.2 A language model's ideas cluster. The authors found that "pools of ideas generated by GPT-4 with various plausible prompts are less diverse than ideas generated by groups of human subjects", and that "Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting leads to the highest diversity of ideas of all prompts we evaluated and was able to come close to what is achieved by groups of human subjects" (`meincke_2024_prompting`, a working paper on one product-design task with one model). What they call chain of thought is a fixed procedure: "Asking GPT-4 to first generate a short list of 100 ideas, then making them bold and different, then generating descriptions for them", the second step reading "go through the list and determine whether the ideas are different and bold, modify the ideas as needed to make them bolder and more different. No two ideas should be the same" (the same source). In the same study, personas such as "Creative Entrepreneur" or "Steve Jobs" moved the ideas' similarity little from the base prompt, and pooling forty separate sessions and refining their best ideas did no better than the base prompt (its Table 2). Three refinements follow for agent helpers:
2.2.1 Follow the tested procedure: a long list of short lines first, by the families of 2.3; then a pass that changes any two that are alike; then the descriptions.
2.2.2 Ask each helper for every method it can see, not only the best one.
2.2.3 A helper's standpoint is kept for what it brings to mind, as in the implementation options, not as a persona that spreads ideas: the study gives no reason to expect the latter.

2.3 The families of method, a checklist so that no kind is missed. The list is this process's synthesis, not any single source's. It does for methods what the Treasury's five ways in which options differ do for implementation options (`author_fast.md` 5.1).
2.3.1 Direct measurement of the quantity in the case's own setting: a study, a statistic, a record.
2.3.2 Analogy: a measurement from another setting, carried over by a stated adjustment. Structured analogies is the established form. Its procedure asks experts "to list as many analogies as they can, rate how similar the analogies are to the target situation, and match the outcomes of the analogies with possible outcomes of the target". In one study, unaided experts were right 32 per cent of the time and structured analogies 46 per cent, and "Among experts who were independently able to think of two or more analogies and who had direct experience with their closest analogy, 60% of forecasts were accurate" (`green_2007_structured`, the 2004 working paper; eight conflict situations).
2.3.3 The outside view: the record of a reference class of comparable cases, rather than the case's own story (`kahneman_1993_timid`). Reference class forecasting "achieves accuracy by basing forecasts on actual performance in a reference class of comparable projects" (`flyvbjerg_2006_nobel`).
2.3.4 Decomposition: built up from parts that are estimated separately, or down from a total of which the quantity is a share. Its value depends on the problem: "decomposition improved accuracy when the problem involved the estimation of extreme and uncertain values. Otherwise, decomposition often produced less accurate predictions" (`macgregor_1994_judgmental`). A decomposed method is therefore most worth building for an input that is extreme or very uncertain, which agrees with the owner's rule 3.
2.3.5 A mechanism: an accounting identity, a physical bound, or a model from first principles. These are the standards the shared core sets for judging any result (CORE rule 2).
2.3.6 For a value: what people do or pay (revealed preference) against what they say (stated preference). This is the field's standard division (`boardman_2018_cost`, recalled, not opened).
2.3.7 Expert judgement that experts have published or given in an elicitation, such as by the IDEA protocol or Cooke's classical model (`notes/what_a_case_delivers.md`, item 4). An agent's own judgement is not this family. It rests on the literature the agent recalls, which the other methods already draw on, so it is independent of none of them (1.2), and the owner asked for approaches "based on known data" (the owner's message of 2026-10-31).

## 3 How to combine them

3.1 The mixture, his first option: each draw picks one method by its weight. This is the linear opinion pool (`genest_1986_combining`, recalled; `clemen_1999_combining`, whose abstract says "no single process is best in all circumstances").
3.1.1 It fits rival accounts of the same quantity, where one of them is likely right and nobody knows which.
3.1.2 It widens the spread: "A key result is, roughly, that dispersion tends to increase under linear pooling", and "Hora (2004) proved that any nontrivial convex combination of two calibrated density forecasts is uncalibrated" (`gneiting_2013_combining`, the arXiv version). Where the methods disagree because their structures differ, that width is the honest reading of the disagreement. Where each method is already well calibrated on the same information, the mixture is too wide.
3.1.3 A mixture also suits two rival estimates far apart, whose overlap would be falsely tight.

3.2 The overlap, his second option, read as the product of the methods' densities, renormalised: the multiplicative pool. It is not the logarithmic pool, whose weights sum to one, p_A^w p_B^(1-w), and which is wider than the plain product; the two are easily confused, and the stored survey keeps them apart, as multiplicative pooling and as "the log-linear pooling function (a weighted geometric mean" (`koliander_2022_fusion`). Of the product, the survey says: "Within the supra-Bayesian framework, the multiplicative pooling function is the correct fusion rule for combining posterior pdfs in the case of conditionally independent observations. In that case, the calibrating pdf q0 is the prior pdf used by the agents to form their posterior pdfs", and next, "Again, this pooling function will not be suitable in most applications" (`koliander_2022_fusion`, the arXiv version).
3.2.1 It fits where each method is an unbiased measure of the same quantity and their errors are independent. It is then Bayes' rule, and the result is narrower than either method alone. Triangulation chooses methods because their biases differ (1.1), so this condition is rarely met unless each method's bias is modelled first (3.5).
3.2.2 A prior the methods share is counted once, divided out of all but one of them.
3.2.3 It fails where one method is wrong. Two densities that barely overlap multiply to a narrow peak between them, a result neither method supports.
3.2.4 This departs from his words. "use where their distributions overlap" is read as this product, the established technique for independent evidence, rather than as truncation to the region both methods find plausible, which is less tight. Truncation has an established form too: history matching, in the calibration of computer models, discards the inputs that any line of evidence finds implausible and refocuses "on the “nonimplausible” regions of the input space" (`vernon_2014_history`). Applied here, it would keep the draws of one method that the other does not rule out. The owner may prefer that reading; it is his to say.

3.3 Weights. "Use equal weights unless you have strong evidence to support unequal weighting of forecasts" (`armstrong_2001_combining`). The same chapter adds that judgmental weights were less accurate than equal weights when those weighting had "poor feedback about the accuracy of the components". In a mixture, equal weight goes to each independent method, and variants of one method share its weight (1.3). For example, four variants of one method among six equally weighted estimates carry two thirds of the weight; weighted by independent method, of which there are three, that family carries one third. A weight other than equal rests on evidence of each method's accuracy, or is the analyst's judgement, marked as such.

3.4 Bayesian model averaging and stacking weight each model by how well it fits observed data. Model averaging is one of the two methods for structural uncertainty in decision models (`bojke_2009_characterizing`). However, "BMA is appropriate for M-closed case. In the M-open and M-complete case, BMA will asymptotically select the one single model in the list that is closest in KullbackLeibler (KL) divergence", where the M-closed case is the one in which the true model is among those fitted (`yao_2018_stacking`). Stacking picks the weights that best predict held-out data, and the authors recommend it for the M-open case (the same source). Both need data against which each method can be scored. A cost-benefit input rarely has such data, so these two apply only where it does, as with a model fitted to a time series.

3.5 Bias modelling, a refinement for the middle tier. Each method's list states the direction of its main errors (`.claude/agents/method-lister.md`). The established use of such statements is to shift and widen each method's distribution by an elicited bias before combining: "Using elicited opinion, we construct prior distributions to represent the biases in each study and perform a bias-adjusted meta-analysis. Adjustment had the effect of shifting the combined estimate away from the null by approximately 10%, and the variance of the combined estimate was almost tripled" (`turner_2009_bias`). It is the next step of triangulation's advice on "being explicit about the expected direction of bias" (`lawlor_2016_triangulation`). It is judgement-heavy, so the fast tier does not do it: the fast author uses the directions only to explain a disagreement (`author_fast.md` 3.7.4). At the middle tier, an author may apply it to an input whose methods disagree beyond their 90 per cent intervals, as a revision the editor's letter asks for; it is also what would make the product of 3.2 usable.

3.6 Never average results. The forecasting literature averages point forecasts (`armstrong_2001_combining`). Rule 9 forbids averaging results, because a point estimate is always wrong (rule 1). The distributional counterpart of an equal-weight average is the equal-weight mixture: its mean is the average of the methods' means, and it keeps their spread.

## 4 What the process adopts

The fast author, for the same inputs it searches (the inputs the design names as setting the main effect, and after the first run the five that drive the result most), follows five steps. The step is `notes/briefs/author_fast.md` 3.7, and its place in the run is `notes/fast_estimate.md` 2.1.
4.1 It writes its own list of methods first.
4.2 Two helpers, blind to each other and to the author's work, each with a different standpoint, work through the families of 2.3.
4.3 The author keeps at least two independent methods per input, by 1.1 and 1.2, and runs each as an input of its own, adjusted to the input's definition where it yields a quantity at another node of the model. The methods the design names are a starting list; a family the helpers name that the design lacks is built or dropped with its reason.
4.4 Where the methods disagree, it looks for the reason before combining.
4.5 It combines them by the mixture of 3.1, with equal weight to each independent method unless there is evidence of accuracy (3.3). The product of 3.2 waits on the owner's ruling of what "overlap" means (3.2.4); until then the brief uses the mixture alone and notes where the methods would qualify for the product.

The middle tier keeps this through its first brief's rules, and applies it to any input the decision letter shows can move the headline (`author_upgrade.md` 3.6.1).
