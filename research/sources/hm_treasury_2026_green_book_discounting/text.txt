Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
The Green Book is the UK government’s guidance on appraisal,
the process of assessing the costs, benefits and risks of different options
for achieving government objectives.1
1.2
The Green Book recommends using discounting to compare
social costs and social benefits occurring over different time periods on
a consistent basis. Discounting enables profiles of social costs and social
benefits stretching over many years to be expressed in ‘present value’
terms.
1.3
This document supplements the Green Book by providing
further guidance on discounting. Annex A sets out the standard
discount factors for appraisal. Practitioners should multiply these by the
expected social costs and social benefits in the relevant years of an
appraisal.

Chapter 2
Derivation of the
discount rate
Introduction
2.1
The discount rate that is used in the Green Book is known
formally as the Social Time Preference Rate (STPR). The STPR is
currently set at 3.00% in real terms for the first 30 years of a proposal.
2.2
The STPR is named as such because it is primarily derived using a
social time preference approach. This is one of the two broad
approaches that can be used to derive the discount rate, which are:
•

Social time preference (STP): The discount rate reflects the rate at
which society is willing to trade off consumption today for
consumption tomorrow.

•

Social opportunity cost of capital (SOC): The discount rate reflects
the cost of capital, derived from the different sources of government
funding, as well as the return on the private investment that it
displaces.

The Ramsey formula
2.3
The STPR is primarily derived using a formula known as the
Ramsey formula. The Ramsey formula is made up of two components
that are added together. These are the:
•

Risk-free discount rate (𝒓𝒇 ): Society’s preference for social benefits
now, rather than later. This is denoted by 𝑟𝑓 .

•

Systematic risk premium (𝑳): The premium that society demands in
compensation for the riskiness of government proposals, and which
arises from the fact that the social benefits of government proposals
generally correlate with the wider economy. This is denoted by the
letter 𝐿.

2.4

The risk-free discount rate is made up of three components:

•

Pure time preference (𝛅): The social time preference that is solely
due to social benefits occurring later in time. This is denoted by the
Greek letter δ (delta).

•

Wealth effect: The social time preference that arises because
society gets richer over time.

2.5

Rare disaster risk (𝑹): The social time preference that arises because
of the risk of a severe, but low-probability, negative shock to
aggregate consumption. This is denoted by the letter 𝑅.
The wealth effect is made up of two components:

•

Elasticity of marginal utility of consumption (µ): An indicator of
how an average person’s wellbeing changes as their consumption
changes. This is denoted by the Greek letter µ (mu).

•

Expected annual growth rate of future real per capita
consumption (𝒈): A forecast of how the average person’s
consumption of goods and services is projected to change over time.
This is denoted by the letter 𝑔.

calculate the wealth effect. This involves multiplying together the

expected annual growth rate of future real per capita consumption (𝑔):

2.10 The second step is to add the value for the wealth effect to the
values for rare disaster risk (𝑅) and pure time preference (𝛿). This gives
an estimate for the risk-free discount rate:
𝛿 + µ𝑔 + 𝑅 = 0.50% + 1.875% − 0.50%

2.11
HM Treasury’s estimate of the risk-free discount rate is primarily
founded on the Ramsey formula. However, it triangulates this estimate
with other evidence, including expert survey data, international
comparisons and the SOC approach. For the SOC approach, HM
Treasury takes into account the inflation-adjusted yields on
government bonds. In June 2026, real yields on 10-year, 20-year and 40year gilts were around 2%.3
2.12 Based on this triangulation, HM Treasury adjusts the risk-free
discount rate upwards by 0.125 percentage points, giving a value of
2.00%:
𝑟𝑓 = 1.875% + 0.125%
𝑟𝑓 = 2.00%

The systematic risk premium
2.13
The STPR approach implies a value for the systematic risk
premium (𝐿) that is around 0.50%.
2.14 As with the risk-free discount rate, HM Treasury informs its
estimate of the systematic risk premium with a triangulation approach.
It corroborates its estimate of 𝐿 with evidence from expert surveys,
international comparisons and the SOC approach. This triangulation
implies a value of 1.00% for the systematic risk premium. The rationale
for this judgment is discussed further in the technical annexes of the
Green Book Discount Rate Review 2026.4

Chapter 3
Discounting in appraisal
Using the discount rate
3.1
Practitioners should use the headline STPR to discount all
proposals. The exception is Official Development Assistance (ODA)
proposals, discussed in Paragraph 3.11.
3.2
Practitioners should not use different discount rates for different
types of proposals. This is because the social value of a proposal should
be reflected in the expected social costs and social benefits, rather than
in its discount rate.
3.3
For example, environmental impacts should generally be
appraised using the STPR. If environmental goods and services are
expected to become scarcer, less substitutable, or more highly valued
over time, then these effects should be reflected through estimates of
future environmental values, rather than through a lower discount rate.
Practitioners should therefore account for such effects through
appropriate relative price adjustments and forecasts of environmental
values, consistent with the relevant Green Book supplementary
guidance.5

Discounting and risk
3.4
The future outcomes of any proposal are uncertain. Practitioners
should generally reflect the possible outcomes in the expected social
costs and social benefits, rather than by varying the discount rate.
3.5
Practitioners should note that the systematic risk premium (𝐿)
does not reflect any obsolescence risk that might be associated with a
proposal. This is the risk that technological advances cause a proposal
to become obsolete and to provide fewer social benefits than expected
over the course of its time horizon. Obsolescence risk should be
reflected in the proposal’s expected social benefits.
3.6
Different proposals may theoretically have different systematic
risk premiums. In other words, their social benefits may have different
degrees of correlation with the overall economy. This means that
different proposals may theoretically have different estimates for the 𝐿
term in the STPR formula. Despite this, in government appraisals,
practitioners should not deviate from the systematic risk premium of
1.00% and hence the STPR of 3.00%.

Proposals with time horizons beyond 30 years
3.7
The standard discount rate of 3.00% declines over time. This is
because there is uncertainty around the future values of the annual
growth rate of real per capita consumption (𝑔) and long-term real gilt
yields, both of which are used in informing the discount rate.
3.8
When conducting an appraisal, practitioners should use a
discount rate of:
•

3.00% for years 1 to 30

•

2.50% for years 31 to 75

•

2.25% for years 76 to 125

3.9
The Green Book discount rate profile is expressed in terms of
forward rates, not spot rates. For example, for an appraisal with a time
horizon ending in year 40, practitioners should use 3.00% as the
discount rate until year 30, then 2.50% from year 31 to year 40. They
should not use 2.50% as the discount rate in each year until year 40.
3.10 Many proposals have social costs and social benefits beyond 125
years. These should be taken into account in appraisal. However,
discounting is not an analytically meaningful technique for social costs
and social benefits that take place after year 125. Practitioners should
therefore use standard social cost-benefit analysis, and the relevant
discount rates, only for monetisable impacts that occur within the first
125 years of an appraisal. For social costs and social benefits that take
place after year 125, practitioners should consider alternative analytical
methods and discuss their approach with HM Treasury.

Official Development Assistance (ODA)
proposals
3.11
The Green Book guidance on discounting may not be suitable for
appraising ODA proposals. Factors such as long-term growth rates can
differ significantly across countries. Consequently, for ODA proposals
where social costs and social benefits primarily affect people in other
countries, alternative discount rates may be appropriate. Practitioners
should contact the Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office
(FCDO) for further guidance.

Discounting and inflation
3.12 Discounting is distinct from adjusting for inflation. The STPR is a
real discount rate and must be applied only to values expressed in real
terms, meaning that the effects of general inflation have already been
removed. Practitioners should first convert social costs and social
benefits into real terms and then discount them using the STPR. The
inflation rate and discount rate should not be added together and
applied to social costs and social benefits because this will produce a
mathematically incorrect result.

Discounting historical costs and benefits
3.13 Discounting should not be applied retrospectively to costs and
benefits that have already occurred. The values of social costs and social
benefits do not increase simply because activities took place in the past.
