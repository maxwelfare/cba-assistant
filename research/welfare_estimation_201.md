# Welfare Estimation 201: its rules, and the literature behind them

Source: Richard Bruns, "Welfare Estimation 201: Understanding Life-Valuation Variability", EA Forum, 10 February 2022, https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/s/4yxZEsjEbPpwpwvy4/p/AMpRKderKGWgQLMqg. Imported 2026-10-31 at his request ("include it in onboarding"). The literature and the verdicts are a referee's (its quotations checked against stored copies of the sources); the reading of what each rule asks here is this session's.

## Summary

A country's value of a statistical life (VSL) is the exchange rate between lives and money for money taken from, or given to, people in that country, and nothing more. Lives count the same wherever they are. So when effects fall on people of different incomes, the analysis counts lives or life-years, and converts each person's money gains and losses into them at that person's own rate: income is worth more lives the poorer its owner. Governments spending their own taxes keep their own country's value; a pool of donor money values lives the same everywhere.

The referee's verdict: sound with changes, a major revision in a working-paper report. The conclusion is right and has firm foundations, which are other people's: Somanathan (2006), Canning (2007, 2013), Fankhauser, Tol and Pearce (1997), and, since the essay, Broome (2024), who makes the same proposal against the EPA's 2023 social cost of carbon.

## The rules, the literature, and what each means here

1. **Count every life, or life-year, equally, wherever it is.** Literature: an axiom, not an implication of nearly all moral theories, as the essay says; a utilitarian welfare function can rank a richer person's life-year higher (Adler, Hammitt and Treich 2014, *Journal of Health Economics* 35:82–93), and the axiom has critics (Baker, Chilton, Jones-Lee and Metcalf 2008, *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* 36:125–138). Somanathan (2006, *Economics Letters* 90(1):122–125) takes it as his first axiom. Whether lives also count the same in every year (no pure time preference on lives) is a separate choice, stated in each case. Here: a comparison across income groups is made in lives or life-years.

2. **Make the life-year the unit, and convert money into it at the rate of whoever gains or loses the money.** The essay's rule of thumb: "increasing the income of 200 people by 1% each is worth a life-year", that is two units of log income per life-year, Open Philanthropy's value; the essay's own preference, "4 units of log income", is 400 people by 1%, and four units over 40 life-years is 160 times income, the reference case's ratio of the US VSL to US income. Literature: Somanathan's weights "proportional to the reciprocals of the values of statistical lives"; Canning (2007, PGDA working paper 2707): "we should always measure willingness to pay in life years, not money units"; Broome (2024, *Journal of Benefit-Cost Analysis* 15(S1):110–126): "A healthy life year is a reasonable, though only approximate, candidate for such a good." Here: dollar damages convert at the local VSL; deaths a source has already valued at local VSLs are counted as deaths; and dividing a dollar present value by today's VSL discounts lives at the discount rate less income growth, which a case that does so says.

3. **Whose rate the results are expressed in changes no ranking; the bar is the best other use of the money.** The essay puts the donor's rate in the role of the bar. Literature: "the set of policies that passes a cost-benefit analysis before and after the normalisation is identical" (Anthoff, Hepburn and Tol 2009, *Ecological Economics* 68:836–849, working paper stored); the bar a funder's option must clear is the opportunity cost of the funds, as in health economics' supply-side thresholds (Claxton et al. 2015, not checked; Ochalek, Lomas and Claxton 2018, *BMJ Global Health* 3:e000964). The essay concedes this twice: cash to the same people is the baseline an intervention must beat, and its $10,000-a-life bar is Open Philanthropy's opportunity-cost bar. Here: a case states its bar as an alternative use of the same money.

4. **A VSL is a welfare equivalence, not a count of deaths.** This departs from the essay, and from his "Mortality Cost of Taxation": the essay's "every (roughly) $10 million of taxation will statistically kill someone" is about tenfold off as a body count. The income loss that causes one death is about $15 million in 1999 (Lutter, Morrall and Viscusi 1999, *Economic Inquiry* 37(4):599–608, when the VSL was about $5 million), and $83 to $134 million, midpoint $108.5 million (Broughel and Viscusi 2021, *Contemporary Economic Policy* 39(1):156–167). The essay's own parenthesis is the reading that holds: lost consumption "would have provided as much happiness or utility as saving a life". Here: never present a VSL conversion as deaths caused.

## 5 What the referee found his own

5.1 The rule of thumb in plain words, 200 people by 1% for a life-year.

5.2 Turning the reference case's own reason for its default against its use for donor money: Robinson, Hammitt and others (2019) justify income-scaled values as addressing "concerns about the resources available for spending on mortality risk reductions in low- and middle-income countries", a reason that assumes the people at risk pay. The referee did not find this in print.

## 6 Where the essay's wording should change, if it is revised

6.1 Cite the foundations (rule 1 to 3 above) in place of "I honestly did try to find someone saying this and failed".

6.2 Drop the body count, "murder" and "dead body on the ground"; rest the argument on welfare equivalence (rule 4).

6.3 State three things apart: the unit (an axiom), the conversion (each person's own rate), the bar (the best other use).

6.4 The reference case's quotation matches its summary word for word, but the essay omits that it recommends cost-effectiveness analysis for reallocating a health budget, which counts DALYs equally, and its footnote saying plainly that one value for all income groups "ignores the variation in the values that each group may place on the risk reductions it receives" (Robinson, Hammitt and O'Keeffe 2019, *Journal of Benefit-Cost Analysis* 10(S1), footnote 8).

6.5 The essay stops at national borders without a reason; the same logic runs within a country.
